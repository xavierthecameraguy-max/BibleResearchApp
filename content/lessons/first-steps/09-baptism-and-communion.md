Jesus did not only give his followers teaching to remember. He gave them actions
to do: being baptised in water, and sharing bread and wine in his memory. Almost
every Christian church practises both, though they understand them in different
ways.

Many churches call these [[sacrament|sacraments]]: holy signs through which God
gives his grace. Catholic and Orthodox churches count seven sacraments, including
these two (the Orthodox often call them holy mysteries). Most Protestant churches recognise these two, and some prefer the word
"ordinances", meaning practices Jesus commanded his people to keep.

## Baptism

At the end of Matthew's Gospel, Jesus tells his disciples to make disciples of all
nations, baptising them in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit
(Matthew 28:19). In our key passage, on the day of Pentecost, Peter calls the
crowd to repent and be baptised in the name of Jesus, and about three thousand
people respond (Acts 2:38-41).

[[baptism|Baptism]] uses water, a basic sign of washing and new life. Paul says
that in baptism believers are joined to Christ's death and resurrection: buried
with him and raised to walk in a new life (Romans 6:3-4). It marks the start of
the Christian life and welcomes a person into the church.

Christians agree on much of this. They differ mainly on two questions: who should
be baptised, and what baptism does.

**Infant baptism.** Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist and Reformed
churches baptise the babies of believing families, as well as adults who come to
faith. They note that whole households were baptised in Acts (Acts 16:15, 33),
that Peter's promise was for "you and your children" (Acts 2:39), and that in the
Old Testament children were included in God's [[covenant]]. Baptising a baby shows
that God's grace reaches us before we can do anything to deserve it. The child is
raised in the faith and later affirms it, often at confirmation. Catholic,
Orthodox and Lutheran Christians, and many Anglicans, believe God truly acts in
baptism, washing away sin and giving new birth by the Spirit. Reformed churches
call it a sign and seal of God's promises, through which the Spirit works in his
own time. Orthodox churches immerse three times, then immediately anoint the
newly baptised (chrismation) and give them communion, even as infants.

**Believer's baptism.** Baptist, Pentecostal, many independent evangelical and
[[anabaptist|Anabaptist]] churches baptise only those who can personally profess
faith. They note that in the New Testament baptism usually follows hearing the
gospel and believing (Acts 2:41; Acts 8:12). Many baptise by full immersion, a
vivid picture of being buried and raised with Christ. For them, baptism is a public
declaration of faith and an act of obedience.

If you were baptised as a child and have now come to faith, churches will respond
differently. Many will treat your baptism as already complete and may invite you to
affirm your faith publicly. Churches that practise believer's baptism will usually
invite you to be baptised now. Talk this over with your pastor or priest.

## Communion

On the night before he died, at what Matthew, Mark and Luke describe as a
[[passover|Passover]] meal (John's Gospel dates it a day earlier), Jesus took bread,
gave thanks, broke it and said it was his body, given for his friends. Then he took
the cup and called it the [[new-covenant|new covenant]] in his blood. He told them
to do this in remembrance of him (Luke 22:19-20; 1 Corinthians 11:23-26). In our
key passage, the first believers devoted themselves to "the breaking of bread"
(Acts 2:42).

This meal has many names: Holy Communion, the Lord's Supper, the
[[eucharist|Eucharist]] (from the Greek for "thanksgiving"), the Mass or the Divine
Liturgy. All agree it remembers Jesus' death, unites believers and looks forward to
his return (1 Corinthians 11:26). They differ on how Christ is present.

- **Catholic:** the bread and wine truly become the body and blood of Christ, while
  still appearing as bread and wine. This is called transubstantiation. The Mass
  makes present Christ's one sacrifice on the cross.
- **Orthodox:** the bread and wine truly become Christ's body and blood. Orthodox
  Christians call this a holy mystery and do not try to define exactly how the
  change happens.
- **Lutheran:** Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with and under" the
  bread and wine.
- **Reformed:** Christ is truly present by the Holy Spirit, who unites believers
  with him so that they are spiritually fed by him.
- **Memorial:** many Baptist and evangelical churches see the bread and wine as
  signs that help us remember and proclaim Christ's death, with Christ present among
  his gathered people.

Anglican and Methodist churches include a range of these views.

## Taking part

Churches have different practices about who may receive communion. Catholic and
Orthodox churches usually ask that only their own members receive, though visitors
are often welcome to come forward for a blessing or, in Orthodox churches, to
receive blessed bread at the end of the service. Many Protestant churches invite
all baptised believers, and some invite anyone who trusts in Jesus.

Paul urges believers to examine themselves before sharing in the meal (1
Corinthians 11:28). This does not mean you must be perfect. It means coming
honestly, ready to receive Jesus' grace.

If you are unsure whether to receive, it is always fine to sit quietly and pray.
Then ask your pastor or priest what their church believes and practises. These are
good conversations to have, and they will be glad you asked.
