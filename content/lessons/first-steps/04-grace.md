Most of life works on a simple rule: you get what you earn. Work hard and you get
paid. Study and you pass the exam. Be kind and people tend to like you.

So it can take a while to get used to the heart of the Christian faith, which
works the other way round.

## What is grace?

[[grace|Grace]] is God's kindness given to people who have not earned it and could
never earn it. It is a gift, not a wage.

Our key passage puts it plainly. Paul reminds his readers that they were once
spiritually dead because of their sins (Ephesians 2:1). Dead people cannot help
themselves. "But God," Paul says, rich in mercy and full of love, made us alive
with Christ (Ephesians 2:4-5). Then come some of the best-loved words in the
Bible. We are saved by grace, through faith. It is not our own achievement but
God's gift, so that nobody can boast (Ephesians 2:8-9).

## Faith: receiving the gift

If grace is God's gift, [[faith]] is the open hand that receives it. Faith is not
a feeling, and it is not a special kind of effort that earns God's approval. It is
trust: relying on Jesus and what he has done, rather than on ourselves.

You do not need great faith. Jesus said that even faith as small as a mustard seed
matters (Matthew 17:20). What saves you is not the size of your faith but the
greatness of the one you are trusting.

## Good works have a place

Does this mean what we do does not matter? Not at all. Ephesians 2:10 follows
straight on: we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus for good works that
God has prepared for us. Good works are not the root of salvation. They are its
fruit. We do not obey God to make him love us. We obey because he already does.

## Forgiveness

Grace brings forgiveness. When we trust in Jesus, God forgives our sins: not just
the small ones, and not only the ones we can remember. The Bible uses vivid
pictures. God removes our sins as far as the east is from the west (Psalm 103:12),
and he promises to remember them no more (Jeremiah 31:34).

Christians still fail after coming to faith. When you do, you do not need to start
over. John promises that if we confess our sins, God is faithful and just to
forgive us (1 John 1:9). Think of Jesus' story of the lost son, whose father runs
to meet him while he is still a long way off (Luke 15:20). That is how God
receives you.

## Can I be sure?

Many new Christians wonder, "What if I'm not really saved?" or "What if I mess it
up?" These are common questions, and good ones to bring to a pastor or mentor.

The New Testament offers real comfort. John wrote his letter so that believers
would know they have eternal life (1 John 5:13). Paul says nothing in all creation
can separate us from God's love in Christ (Romans 8:38-39). Jesus said no one can
snatch his sheep out of his hand (John 10:28).

## How traditions describe it

All the main Christian traditions agree that [[salvation]] begins with God, that
it comes through Jesus, and that no one can earn it. They describe how it works,
and how sure we can be, in different ways.

**Protestant** churches emphasise [[justification]] by grace through faith alone:
God declares us right with him because of Christ, not because of anything we do.
Many speak confidently of assurance. Some, especially in the Reformed tradition,
teach that God will keep every true believer to the end. Others, such as many
Methodists, stress that believers can know they are forgiven now, while urging
them to keep walking in faith.

**Catholic** teaching also says salvation is entirely God's gift of grace,
received through faith and [[baptism]]. It understands justification as God not
only forgiving us but truly making us new within, so that by his grace we can
cooperate with him. Catholics speak of a firm and confident hope in God's mercy,
and warn against both despair and taking God for granted. The
[[sacrament]] of reconciliation, or confession, is a gift for receiving
forgiveness after baptism.

**Orthodox** Christians describe salvation as a lifelong journey of healing and
growing union with God, made possible entirely by grace, with our free response,
and lived out in the church and its sacraments, which they call holy mysteries.
Many Orthodox would say, "I have been saved, I am being saved, and I hope to be
saved." They rest their hope in God's great mercy rather than in a single moment.

These differences are real and worth exploring with your church. But they share a
common heart: you are loved before you have done anything, and God himself is the
one who saves.

## Living from grace

Grace changes how you live. You do not have to perform for God. You can be honest
about your failures. You can rest. And, freed from trying to earn love, you can
begin to give it away.
