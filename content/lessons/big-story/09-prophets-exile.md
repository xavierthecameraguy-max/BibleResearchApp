Last time we watched Israel rise to its high point under David and Solomon, and
heard God's great promise that David's family would reign forever. Then, around
930 BC, the kingdom split in two: Israel in the north and Judah in the south.

This lesson covers several centuries, and some of the most painful chapters in
Israel's history.

## Messengers from God

Over the next few centuries, many kings in both kingdoms turn away from
God. They worship other gods, ignore the poor and treat the Law as optional. So
God sends [[prophet|prophets]].

People often think a prophet is mainly someone who predicts the future. That is
part of it, but it is not the main thing. A biblical prophet is God's
messenger, sent to speak to his own generation. Prophets were a bit like
covenant lawyers. They reminded Israel of the promises made at Sinai, warned of
the consequences of breaking them, and called the people to turn back to God.

Some prophets, like Elijah and Elisha, appear in the stories of 1 and 2 Kings.
Others have books named after them, such as Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Hosea,
Amos and Micah. Their messages often have two sides. First comes warning: if
you keep going this way, disaster will come. Then comes hope: after judgement,
God will not abandon you, and he will make things new.

The prophets were passionate about justice. Amos thunders against those who
trample the poor. Micah sums up what God wants: doing what is fair, loving
faithful kindness, and walking humbly with God (Micah 6:8). Hosea compares Israel to an unfaithful
spouse whom God still loves and wants back.

## The fall of the north

The northern kingdom of Israel never had a faithful king, according to the books
of Kings. In 722 BC the mighty Assyrian empire conquered its capital, Samaria,
and scattered many of its people across the empire (2 Kings 17). The ten northern
tribes largely disappear from the story.

## The fall of Jerusalem

The southern kingdom of Judah lasts longer. It has some good kings, such as
Hezekiah and Josiah, who lead reforms. But the overall direction is downward.

Jeremiah prophesies in Jerusalem for about forty years, warning that the
Babylonians are coming. People mock him, beat him and throw him into a muddy
well. Then it happens. In 587 or 586 BC (scholars differ by a year), the
Babylonian army under Nebuchadnezzar destroys Jerusalem, burns Solomon's temple,
and carries many of the people away to Babylon (2 Kings 25).

This is the [[exile]]. It is hard to overstate how devastating it was. The land
promised to Abraham is lost. The king from David's line is in chains. The temple,
where God's presence lived, is rubble. It feels as if the whole story has
collapsed. The book of Lamentations is a collection of poems of grief over this
moment.

## Hope in a foreign land

Yet the exile is also the setting for some of the Bible's most hopeful words.
Prophets such as Jeremiah, Ezekiel and parts of Isaiah speak of a future
beyond judgement. (Many scholars think Isaiah 40–55 was written during the exile
by a later prophet in Isaiah's tradition; others hold that the whole book comes
from Isaiah himself, in the 700s BC.)

Jeremiah tells the exiles to settle down, build houses and seek the good of
Babylon, the city where they now live (Jeremiah 29:4-7). Then he gives one of the
greatest promises in the Old Testament. God will make a [[new-covenant|new
covenant]]. Unlike the covenant at Sinai, which the people broke, this one will be
written on their hearts. Everyone will know God, and he will forgive their sins
and remember them no more (Jeremiah 31:31-34).

Ezekiel has a vision of a valley full of dry bones that God brings back to life
(Ezekiel 37), and promises that God will give his people a new heart and put his
Spirit within them (Ezekiel 36:26-27). Isaiah speaks of a coming servant who will
suffer for the sins of others (Isaiah 52:13–53:12). Earlier chapters of Isaiah
also promise a child from David's line who will rule with justice forever
(Isaiah 9:6-7; 11:1-9).

Notice how these promises connect back. A king from David's family. A heart
renewed. Sins forgiven. Blessing for the nations. The prophets are saying that
God's plan has not failed. It is going deeper.

## Coming home

In 539 BC the Persian king Cyrus conquers Babylon. He allows exiled peoples to
return to their homelands, and groups of Jews begin making their way back to
Judah (Ezra 1). They rebuild the temple, which is completed around 516 BC. Later,
in the 400s BC, Ezra calls the people back to the Law and Nehemiah leads the
rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls.

But the return feels incomplete. The new temple is far less splendid than Solomon's, and some older people who
remember the first temple weep when its foundations are laid (Ezra 3:12). The Jews are home, but
they are still ruled by foreign empires. There is no king from David's line on the
throne. The great promises have not yet come true.

## Four hundred years of waiting

In Protestant and most modern Catholic Bibles the Old Testament ends with the
prophet Malachi, who probably spoke in the 400s BC. (Jewish and Orthodox Bibles arrange
the books in a different order.) The roughly four hundred years between
Malachi and Jesus are often called the
[[intertestamental-period|intertestamental period]], or simply "the four hundred
years". History did not
stop. The Persians were replaced by the Greeks under Alexander the Great in the
330s BC, then a Jewish revolt led by the Maccabees, beginning in 167 BC, won back the
temple and later independence for a time, and the Romans took control in 63 BC.

Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include books from this period, such as 1 and 2
Maccabees, which they call [[deuterocanonical]] books (Orthodox Bibles include a few more
than Catholic ones). Protestants usually call
them the [[apocrypha|Apocrypha]] and do not count them as Scripture, though many
find them helpful for history.

Through all those years, many Jews waited and prayed. They longed for God to send
his Messiah, defeat their enemies, return to his temple and set the world right.

## What comes next

The stage is set. The promises to Abraham, Moses and David are still waiting to be
fulfilled. In the next lesson the waiting ends, as a child is born in Bethlehem,
the town of David.
