Last time we saw the first humans turn from God in the garden, and the damage
that followed: shame, blame, pain and death. We also saw a glimmer of hope, a
promise that evil would one day be crushed.

In this lesson we cover a lot of ground, Genesis 4 to 11. These chapters move
quickly. They show sin spreading like a weed across the early world, and God
responding with both judgement and mercy.

## From a garden to a murder

Genesis 4 introduces Adam and Eve's sons, Cain and Abel. Both bring offerings to
God. God accepts Abel's but not Cain's, and Cain grows angry and jealous. God
warns him that sin is crouching at his door, wanting to control him, but that he
can master it (Genesis 4:7). Cain does not listen. He lures his brother into a
field and kills him.

In just one generation, mistrust of God has turned into murder. When God asks
where Abel is, Cain answers with a famous dodge: am I my brother's keeper?
(Genesis 4:9). The honest answer, of course, is yes.

Even so, God shows mercy. He puts a mark on Cain to protect him from revenge
(Genesis 4:15). The pattern from the garden continues: judgement is real, but God
keeps caring for the people who wrong him.

## A world full of violence

Genesis 5 is a family tree. It can feel dry, but it carries a quiet message. Again
and again it ends with "and he died", a reminder of what was lost in the garden.
One man, Enoch, stands out because he "walked with God" (Genesis 5:24), a
beautiful phrase for a life of close friendship with God.

By Genesis 6 things are very bad. The earth is filled with violence, and human
hearts are bent toward evil all the time (Genesis 6:5, 11). The text says
something striking: God is grieved, deeply hurt in his heart (Genesis 6:6). This
is not a cold, distant God. He is like a parent watching their children destroy
themselves and each other.

## The flood

God decides to wash the world clean with a great flood. But one man, Noah, finds
favour with God (Genesis 6:8). God tells Noah to build a large boat, the ark, and
to bring his family and pairs of animals on board.

The flood comes, and the waters cover the earth. In a sense, creation is being
undone. In Genesis 1 God separated the waters to make dry land. Now the waters
come back together. But inside the ark, life is kept safe. When the waters go
down, Noah steps out onto a fresh, clean world, almost like a second creation.

People ask whether the flood covered the whole planet or a large region. Here
too, Christians hold different views, and scholars note that other ancient
peoples, such as the Babylonians, told flood stories of their own. What makes
the Bible's version distinctive is its focus: a just God who grieves over evil,
and a merciful God who preserves life.

## God's covenant with Noah

When Noah comes out of the ark, he builds an altar and offers a [[sacrifice]], a
gift offered to God in worship. Then God makes a promise. He will never again
destroy all life with a flood (Genesis 9:11).

This is the first [[covenant]] named in the Bible. A covenant is a solemn,
committed relationship sealed with promises. Covenants are a big deal in the
Bible's story: God makes several of them, and each one moves the plan forward.
You will meet them again and again in this track.

The covenant with Noah is remarkably wide. It is made not only with Noah's
family but with every living creature and with the earth itself (Genesis 9:9-10).
God gives it a sign: his bow in the clouds, the rainbow (Genesis 9:13). Some
scholars point out that the word for "bow" is the same word used for a warrior's
bow. It is as if God hangs up his weapon in the sky, pointed away from the earth.

God also repeats the blessing from Genesis 1: be fruitful and fill the earth
(Genesis 9:1). And he restates that every human life is precious, because people
are made in his [[image-of-god|image]] (Genesis 9:6).

## The flood did not fix the human heart

You might expect a happy ending here. But soon after the flood, Noah gets drunk
and his family falls into shame and conflict (Genesis 9:20-27). The water washed
the world, but it could not wash the human heart. God even says as much: human
hearts are still inclined toward evil (Genesis 8:21). The problem of sin goes
deeper than any outward clean-up can reach.

## The tower of Babel

The final story in this section is short but important (Genesis 11:1-9). People
settle together on a plain and decide to build a city with a tower reaching to
the heavens. Their goal is to make a name for themselves and avoid being
scattered.

Remember, God had told humans to fill the earth. Here they refuse, huddling
together to build a monument to their own greatness. It is the garden all over
again: humans trying to reach God's place by their own power.

God comes down (a gentle bit of humour, since their mighty tower is so small that
he has to come down to see it) and confuses their language. Unable to understand
one another, they scatter across the earth. The place is called Babel, which
sounds like a Hebrew word for confusion.

## A scattered world waiting for a plan

By the end of Genesis 11, the world is full of nations divided by language,
pride and violence. The human family is broken into pieces. What will God do now?

His answer is surprising. Instead of starting over again, he chooses one man and
his family, and through them he plans to bless every scattered nation. In the
next lesson we will meet that man: Abraham.
