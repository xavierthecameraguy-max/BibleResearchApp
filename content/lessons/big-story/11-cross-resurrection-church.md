Last time we met Jesus, the King Israel had waited for. He announced the kingdom
of God, called disciples, healed the sick and taught in parables. But he kept
telling his followers something they did not want to hear: the King would suffer
and die.

In this lesson we come to the centre of the whole Bible.

## The last Passover

Jesus travels to Jerusalem for the [[passover|Passover]] festival, when Jews
remembered God's rescue from Egypt. He rides into the city on a donkey, a
deliberate sign recalling a promise of a humble king (Zechariah 9:9), and crowds
welcome him. He then clears out the merchants in the temple courts, angering the
authorities.

On the night before he dies, Jesus shares a Passover meal with his disciples,
known as the Last Supper. (Matthew, Mark and Luke present it as a Passover meal.
John's Gospel seems to place it a day earlier, and scholars still discuss how
the accounts fit together.) He takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it and says it is
his body, given for them. Then he takes a cup of wine and says it is the
[[new-covenant|new covenant]] in his blood (Luke 22:19-20). Remember Jeremiah's
promise of a new covenant, with sins forgiven and hearts renewed? Jesus says it
begins with his death.

Christians still share this meal, calling it Communion, the Lord's Supper or the
[[eucharist|Eucharist]] (from the Greek for "thanksgiving"). Christians understand
what happens in it in different ways. Catholic and Orthodox Christians believe
the bread and wine truly become Christ's body and blood. Many Protestants also
speak of Christ being truly present, though they explain it in different ways,
while others see the meal mainly as a memorial. Many count it as a
[[sacrament]]. All see it as a meal Jesus gave his followers to share in
remembrance of him (1 Corinthians 11:23-26).

## The cross

That night Jesus is arrested. One of his disciples, Judas, betrays him, and Peter
denies even knowing him. Jesus is put on trial before the Jewish council and then
before the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate. Pilate sentences him to crucifixion, a
brutal Roman execution, and he dies on a cross outside Jerusalem. Most scholars
date this to about AD 30 or AD 33.

Why would God's chosen King die like a criminal? The New Testament gives a
surprising answer: this was not a defeat but the heart of God's plan. Through his
death, Jesus deals with sin, the problem that began in the garden. This is called
the [[atonement]], God making us "at one" with him again. The Bible describes it
with several pictures: a [[sacrifice]] like the Passover lamb, a ransom that sets
slaves free, a victory over evil, and Jesus taking our place. Christians
emphasise these pictures differently, but all agree that the cross shows God's
love (Romans 5:8).

## The empty tomb

Jesus' body is placed in a tomb, and a large stone is rolled across the entrance.
Early on the third day, Sunday, several women go to the tomb and find it empty.
Angels tell them that Jesus is not there; he has risen (Luke 24:1-8).

Over the following weeks, Jesus appears many times to his followers. He walks
with two of them on the road to Emmaus and explains how all the Scriptures, from
Moses through the prophets, point to him (Luke 24:13-35). He eats with the
disciples and invites them to touch him. Paul later lists many witnesses,
including a group of more than five hundred (1 Corinthians 15:3-8).

This is the [[resurrection]]. It does not mean Jesus' spirit floated away, or
that he came back to his old life only to die again. It means he was raised bodily
to a new life that death can never touch. Christians see it as the first piece of
God's new world arriving early, and the promise that those who belong to Jesus
will one day be raised too.

## The Spirit comes

Before returning to the Father, Jesus tells his followers to wait in Jerusalem for
the [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]], and promises they will be his witnesses there, in the
surrounding regions, and to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8). That verse is a kind
of map for the book of Acts.

About ten days after Jesus returns to the Father (his [[ascension]]), at the
Jewish festival of [[pentecost|Pentecost]], the Spirit comes
on the believers with the sound of wind and what look like flames (Acts 2). They
begin speaking in many languages, and visitors from across the world hear about
God in their own tongues. Do you remember Babel, where languages divided
humanity? Here God uses languages to gather people together.

Peter stands up and preaches that the Jesus who was crucified is Lord and
Messiah. About three thousand people believe and are baptised. The
[[church]] is born. In the Bible, "church" means not a building but a gathered
people. These first believers share meals, pray, care for the poor and meet
together daily (Acts 2:42-47).

## The good news goes to the nations

At first the believers are almost all Jewish. But God's promise to Abraham was to
bless all the nations. In Acts 10, Peter is sent to the home of Cornelius, a Roman
soldier, and the Spirit comes on these [[gentile|Gentiles]], people who are not
Jewish. The church has to work out what this means. At a meeting in Jerusalem
(Acts 15), the leaders agree that Gentiles are welcomed into God's people by
grace through faith in Jesus, without first having to become Jewish.

A key figure is Paul. He was a devout Jewish teacher who persecuted the early
believers, until the risen Jesus confronted him on the road to Damascus, probably
in the early to mid 30s AD (Acts 9). Paul becomes an [[apostle]], one sent out
with the message. Over the next twenty years or so he travels across the Roman
world, planting churches in cities such as Corinth, Ephesus and Philippi, and
writing letters that are now part of the New Testament. Acts ends with Paul in
Rome, still preaching. Early tradition says he was executed there in the
mid-60s AD.

## What comes next

The good news has begun its journey to the ends of the earth, and it continues
today. But the story is not finished. In our final lesson we will look at how the
Bible ends, with God making all things new.
