Last time we watched God rescue Israel from slavery in Egypt. Through the
plagues, the Passover and the crossing of the sea, God showed his power and his
faithfulness. But rescue was only the beginning.

In the third month after leaving Egypt, the Israelites arrive at Mount Sinai,
in the desert (Exodus 19:1). There God tells them why he brought them out: he carried them, like an eagle
carrying its young, and brought them to himself (Exodus 19:4).

## A covenant at the mountain

At Sinai, God makes a [[covenant]] with Israel. You met this word with Noah and
Abraham: a solemn, committed relationship sealed with promises. Here it is like
a marriage. God commits himself to Israel as their God, and Israel commits to
live as his people.

God gives them a calling: they will be his treasured possession, a kingdom of
priests and a holy nation (Exodus 19:5-6). Priests stand between God and others.
So Israel's job is to show the surrounding nations what God is like. This is the
promise to Abraham at work: through this family, God plans to bless the world.

## The Ten Commandments

Then, with thunder, fire and smoke on the mountain, God speaks the Ten
Commandments (Exodus 20:1-17). In short, they say:

1. Have no other gods besides the LORD.
2. Do not make idols to worship.
3. Do not misuse God's name.
4. Keep the Sabbath day holy.
5. Honour your father and mother.
6. Do not murder.
7. Do not commit adultery.
8. Do not steal.
9. Do not give false testimony.
10. Do not covet what belongs to your neighbour.

(This list follows the numbering used by Orthodox, Anglican and Reformed
Christians. Jewish, Catholic and Lutheran traditions divide the same commands
slightly differently, so the numbering can vary. The content is the same.)

Look carefully at what comes before the list. God begins: I am the LORD your God,
who brought you out of Egypt, out of slavery (Exodus 20:2). The order matters.
God does not say, "Keep these rules and I will rescue you." He says, "I have
rescued you, so now live like free people." Obedience is a grateful response to
grace, not a way of earning it.

## The Law as a gift

The commandments are followed by many more instructions, which continue through
Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. Together these are called the [[law|Law]], or
in Hebrew the [[torah|Torah]], which means "instruction" or "teaching".

To modern readers the Law can seem strange, with rules about food, farming,
clothing and offerings. Some parts are hard to understand without knowing the
ancient world. But Israel saw the Law as a gift. Psalm 19 calls it more precious
than gold and sweeter than honey. It taught them how to love God and love their
neighbour, how to treat the poor, the foreigner and the widow fairly, and how to
deal with sin through [[sacrifice]] and forgiveness.

Christians differ on exactly how the Law applies today, but they agree that Jesus
summed it up in two commands: love God with all you are, and love your
neighbour as yourself (Matthew 22:37-40).

## God moves in

One of the most surprising parts of Exodus is the long section, chapters
25–40, about building a tent. God tells Moses: have them make a sanctuary for
me, so that I can live among them (Exodus 25:8).

This tent is the [[tabernacle]]. It is portable, so it can travel with the people.
It has courtyards, curtains, an altar, a lampstand, and an inner room called the
Most Holy Place, where the ark of the covenant sits. Many readers notice that
its tree-like lampstand, its flower and fruit designs, and its cherubim (the
guardians of Eden in Genesis 3:24) echo the garden of Eden.

Remember how the first humans were sent out of the garden, away from God's
presence? Now, in a small way, God is coming back to live with his people. When
the tabernacle is finished, God's glory fills it like a cloud (Exodus 40:34).
This theme, God dwelling with his people, runs all the way to the last page of the
Bible.

## The golden calf

Yet right in the middle of these instructions, disaster strikes. While Moses is
on the mountain, the people grow impatient. They ask Moses' brother Aaron to make
them a god, and he shapes a golden calf. They worship it and say this is the god
who brought them out of Egypt (Exodus 32:1-6).

It is like a bride being unfaithful on her wedding day. The very first
commandments are broken almost at once. God's anger is real, and there are
serious consequences. But Moses pleads for the people, and God listens.

Then God reveals himself to Moses with words that are repeated across the
Bible: the LORD is compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, and overflowing
with faithful love, forgiving sin, yet not ignoring guilt (Exodus 34:6-7). God
renews the covenant. He does not abandon his people, even at their worst.

## Forty years in the wilderness

From Sinai the people set off toward the promised land. But the journey goes
badly. They complain about food and water, and God provides manna, bread from
heaven, and water from a rock. When scouts report that the land's inhabitants
are strong, the people refuse to trust God and want to go back to Egypt
(Numbers 13–14).

As a result, that generation wanders in the wilderness for forty years. Only
their children will enter the land. The book of Deuteronomy records Moses'
final speeches as the new generation stands at the edge of Canaan. He urges
them to love God with all their heart and to choose life (Deuteronomy 30:19-20).
Moses himself dies on a mountain, looking over the land he will not enter.

## What this means for us

Sinai shows that God does not just rescue people and leave them. He wants a
relationship. He gives instruction for how to live well, and he wants to live
with us. And when we fail, as Israel did so quickly, he remains gracious.

In the next lesson we will see the new generation finally cross into the
promised land under Joshua, and then struggle to stay faithful in the days of
the judges.
