Last time we followed Abraham's family through Isaac, Jacob and Joseph. Genesis
ended with them living safely in Egypt, rescued from famine through Joseph.
God's promise was alive, but they were far from the land he had promised.

The book of Exodus picks up the story many years later, and things have changed.

## From guests to slaves

Abraham's descendants, now called the Israelites, have grown into a large
people. A new Pharaoh comes to power who does not remember Joseph (Exodus 1:8).
He fears that the Israelites might turn against Egypt, so he forces them into
brutal slave labour. When that does not work, he orders that Hebrew baby boys be
killed.

It is a dark moment. The family that was meant to bless the nations is being
crushed by one of them. And yet the first chapter quietly shows God at work:
two midwives, Shiphrah and Puah, bravely refuse Pharaoh's order (Exodus 1:15-21).

## Moses and the burning bush

One Hebrew mother hides her baby, then places him in a basket among the reeds of
the Nile. Pharaoh's own daughter finds him, adopts him and names him Moses. He
grows up in the palace, but when he kills an Egyptian who is beating a Hebrew
slave, he flees into the desert.

Years later, while caring for sheep, Moses sees a bush that is on fire but not
burning up. From the bush God speaks. He says he has seen his people's misery
and heard their cry, and he has come down to rescue them (Exodus 3:7-8). He is
sending Moses to Pharaoh.

Moses asks God's name. God answers, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). This name,
linked to the [[hebrew|Hebrew]] verb "to be", is God's personal name. Many English Bibles
print it as "the LORD" in small capitals. It suggests a God who simply is, who
depends on nothing, and who will truly be present with his people.

## The plagues

Moses goes to Pharaoh with a simple message from God: let my people go. Pharaoh
refuses. What follows are ten plagues. The first nine are the Nile turning to
blood, frogs, gnats, flies, disease on livestock, boils, hail, locusts and
darkness.

The plagues are more than disasters. Egypt worshipped many gods connected with
the Nile, the sun, the land and Pharaoh himself. God says he is bringing
judgement on all the gods of Egypt (Exodus 12:12), and taken together the
plagues show that Israel's God is Lord over all of them. It is a contest of
power, and only one God is truly God.

## The Passover

The tenth plague is the most terrible: the death of every firstborn in Egypt.
But God gives Israel a way to be spared. Each household is to sacrifice a lamb,
put its blood on the doorframe, and eat a special meal ready to travel. When the
plague comes, it will pass over the marked homes (Exodus 12:1-13).

That night is remembered as the [[passover|Passover]]. God tells Israel to
celebrate it every year so that every generation remembers how God rescued them.
Jewish families still keep Passover today. Keep this meal in mind, because many
centuries later Jesus will eat a Passover meal with his friends on the night
before he dies.

## Through the sea

After the final plague, Pharaoh finally lets the people go. But soon he changes
his mind and chases them with his army. The Israelites are trapped between the
sea and the soldiers. They panic, but Moses tells them not to be afraid: the LORD
will fight for them, and they need only be still (Exodus 14:13-14).

God sends a strong wind that drives back the waters, and the people cross on dry
ground. When the Egyptian army follows, the waters return. On the far shore,
Moses and his sister Miriam lead the people in songs of praise (Exodus 15).

Do you notice the echo? In creation, God separated the waters so dry land could
appear. Now he does it again, bringing his people out of death into new life. The
exodus is like a new creation for a new people.

## When did it happen?

It is fair to ask when this took place. Scholars do not agree. Some date the
exodus to about 1446 BC, the 15th century, based on a number in 1 Kings 6:1.
Many others prefer a date around 1250 BC, the 13th century, partly because
Exodus 1:11 mentions a city called Rameses, a name linked to later pharaohs.
Many scholars also think any historical event behind the story was much
smaller than a straightforward reading suggests, perhaps a small group leaving
Egypt, and some doubt it happened as described at all. Archaeology has not
settled the question. Christians hold a range
of views on these details while agreeing on the heart of the story.

## The Bible's great picture of rescue

The word [[exodus]] means "going out". This event becomes the Bible's main
picture of salvation. Israel's later prophets, poets and leaders look back to it
constantly. When they are in trouble, they pray, in effect: God, do it again.

Notice the shape of the rescue. The Israelites did not free themselves. They did
not earn it by being good. God heard their cry and acted out of love and
faithfulness to his promise to Abraham (Exodus 2:24). The New Testament will
describe what Jesus does in the same terms: a rescue from slavery, this time
slavery to sin and death.

## What comes next

Rescue is not the end of the story. God did not bring Israel out of Egypt simply
to leave them in the desert. He brought them out to bring them to himself. In
the next lesson we will travel with them to Mount Sinai, where God makes a
covenant with them and comes to live among them.
