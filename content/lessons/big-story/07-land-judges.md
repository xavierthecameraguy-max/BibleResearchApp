Last time we stood at Mount Sinai, where God made a covenant with Israel, gave
them his Law and came to live among them in the tabernacle. Then a generation
wandered in the wilderness for forty years. Now a new generation stands at the
edge of the land God promised to Abraham centuries before.

## Joshua and the land

Moses has died, and leadership passes to his assistant, Joshua. God tells him to
be strong and courageous, because God will be with him just as he was with Moses
(Joshua 1:9). Joshua's name, by the way, means "the LORD saves". In Greek it is
the same name as Jesus.

The book of Joshua opens with echoes of the exodus. The people cross the Jordan
River on dry ground, just as their parents crossed the sea (Joshua 3). Then comes
the famous story of Jericho, whose walls fall after the people march around the
city (Joshua 6). Over time, the tribes settle in different parts of the land.

## A hard part of the story

We should be honest: the conquest stories in Joshua are among the hardest parts
of the Bible for modern readers. They describe warfare and the destruction of
Canaanite cities. Christians wrestle with these texts in different ways. Some
stress that the Bible presents this as God's judgement on deeply violent
cultures after long patience (Genesis 15:16). Many scholars note that ancient
war reports often used exaggerated, sweeping language, and point out that
Judges describes many Canaanites still living in the land. Historians and
archaeologists also debate how the settlement actually unfolded. It is fine to
find these chapters difficult, and to keep reading and asking questions.

The book of Joshua also includes surprising mercy. Rahab, a Canaanite woman in
Jericho, trusts Israel's God, and she and her family are welcomed into Israel
(Joshua 2; 6:25). Her faith matters more than her background.

Near the end of his life, Joshua gathers the people and challenges them to
choose whom they will serve. He declares that he and his household will serve
the LORD (Joshua 24:15). The people renew their [[covenant]] promises to do the same (Joshua 24:24-25). Sadly, the promise
does not last long.

## The days of the judges

After Joshua's generation dies, a new generation grows up who do not know the
LORD or what he has done (Judges 2:10). The book of Judges, covering roughly
1200–1050 BC, tells what happens next.

Judges 2 lays out a pattern that repeats through the book:

1. **Turning away.** Israel worships the gods of the surrounding peoples.
2. **Trouble.** God lets enemies oppress them.
3. **Crying out.** In their distress, the people call out to God.
4. **Rescue.** God raises up a leader, called a judge, to deliver them.
5. **Rest.** There is peace while the judge lives, and then the cycle begins again.

A "judge" here does not mean someone in a courtroom. These are leaders, often
military heroes, whom God raises up to rescue his people. They include Deborah, a
wise prophetess and leader (Judges 4–5); Gideon, a fearful farmer who becomes a
brave commander (Judges 6–8); and Samson, an extremely strong man with serious
weaknesses (Judges 13–16).

## A downward spiral

The cycle is not just a circle. It is a spiral heading down. Each judge seems
more flawed than the last. Gideon ends up making a gold object that the people worship (Judges 8:27). Samson is reckless and
self-centred. By the final chapters, the stories are truly disturbing, showing
the violence and chaos that follow when a people forgets God.

The book keeps repeating one sad line: in those days Israel had no king, and
everyone did what was right in their own eyes (Judges 21:25). Do you remember
the garden, where the first humans chose to decide good and evil for themselves?
Judges shows what a whole society looks like when everyone does that.

Yet the most striking thing in Judges is God's patience. Every time the people
cry out, he answers. He is grieved by their misery (Judges 10:16). He keeps
rescuing a people who keep wandering. This is what [[repentance]], turning back
to God, looks like, even when it is shallow, and what God's mercy looks like in
return.

## Ruth: light in a dark time

Right after Judges, Christian Bibles place a short and beautiful book: Ruth.
(Jewish Bibles keep it in a later section called the Writings.) It is set
"in the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1), but it feels completely
different.

An Israelite woman named Naomi loses her husband and both sons while living in
Moab, a neighbouring nation. Her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth refuses to leave
her. Ruth makes a moving promise: she will go wherever Naomi goes, Naomi's people
will become her people, and Naomi's God will be her God (Ruth 1:16).

Back in Bethlehem, Ruth gathers leftover grain in the fields of a kind relative
named Boaz. Boaz protects and provides for her, and in the end he marries her.
There are no battles or miracles, just ordinary faithfulness and kindness. Yet
God is quietly at work. Ruth's son Obed becomes the grandfather of David, Israel's
greatest king (Ruth 4:17).

Notice too that Ruth is a foreigner. Through her loyalty, a woman from outside
Israel becomes part of the family line that leads to the king. It is a small
taste of the promise to Abraham: blessing for all the nations.

## What comes next

By the end of Judges, Israel is crying out for order. The line "Israel had no
king" hangs in the air, and Ruth ends by naming David. In the next lesson the
people ask for a king, and we meet Samuel, Saul, David and Solomon.
