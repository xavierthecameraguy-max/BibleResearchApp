Last time we saw Israel settle in the promised land under Joshua, then slide into
the chaotic cycle of the judges. The book of Judges kept repeating that Israel
had no king and everyone did whatever they thought was right. Then the little
book of Ruth ended with a name: David.

In this lesson, Israel gets its kings. The story is told in 1 and 2 Samuel and 1
Kings, with another telling in 1 and 2 Chronicles.

## Samuel: the bridge

The story begins with a woman named Hannah, who cannot have children. She pours
out her heart to God, and he gives her a son, Samuel (1 Samuel 1). Samuel grows up
serving at the tabernacle, and God calls him as a young boy (1 Samuel 3).

Samuel becomes the last of the judges and also a [[prophet]], someone who speaks
God's message to the people. He leads Israel faithfully. But as he grows old, the
people come to him with a request: give us a king, like all the other nations
(1 Samuel 8:5).

## Asking for a king

Samuel is upset, and God tells him the people are not really rejecting Samuel
but God himself as their king (1 Samuel 8:7). Still, God allows it. Samuel warns
them that kings will take their sons, their fields and their money. The people
insist.

This is a mixed moment. Israel was always meant to have a king one day
(Deuteronomy 17:14-20 even gives rules for kings). The problem is their reason.
They want to be like the other nations, when God called them to be different.

## Saul: a promising start

God chooses Saul, a tall, impressive young man, as the first king, around 1050
BC. He starts well and wins battles. But Saul turns out to care more about his
image than about obeying God. He offers a sacrifice he was told to wait for
(1 Samuel 13) and keeps plunder he was told to destroy (1 Samuel 15). Samuel tells
him that God values obedience more than sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22).

God rejects Saul's kingship, and Saul's later life becomes a sad story of
jealousy, fear and decline.

## David: a heart for God

God sends Samuel to Bethlehem, to the family of Jesse, Ruth's grandson. Jesse's
older sons look like kings, but God tells Samuel that he does not look at outward
appearance. He looks at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). God chooses the youngest, a
shepherd boy named David, and Samuel anoints him with oil.

You may know what happens next: David faces the giant Goliath with only a sling,
trusting God for victory (1 Samuel 17). Saul becomes jealous and hunts David for
years, yet David refuses to harm "the LORD's anointed" king.

David becomes king around 1010 BC. He captures Jerusalem and makes it his
capital, then brings the ark of the covenant there. He is also remembered as a
songwriter: 73 of the Psalms, the songs and prayers of the Bible, carry his
name in their headings, though scholars debate how many he wrote himself.

Outside the Bible, an inscription from the 800s BC, found at Tel Dan in northern
Israel, mentions the "house of David". Archaeologists still debate how large and
powerful the kingdoms of David and Solomon actually were.

## God's promise to David

Once settled, David wants to build a permanent house for God, a temple. But God
turns the idea around. Speaking through the prophet Nathan, God says that David
will not build God a house. Instead, God will build David a "house", meaning a
royal family line (2 Samuel 7:11). One of David's descendants will build the
temple, and God promises that David's throne will be established forever (2
Samuel 7:16).

This is the [[davidic-covenant|Davidic covenant]], and it is one of the most
important promises in the Bible. From now on, Israel hopes for a great king from
David's line who will rule with justice and bring God's blessing. Over time that
hoped-for king comes to be called the [[messiah|Messiah]], the "anointed one".
Keep that word in mind. The New Testament opens by calling Jesus the son of David
(Matthew 1:1).

## David's failure

David is not perfect. At the height of his power, he takes another man's wife,
Bathsheba, and arranges for her husband Uriah to be killed in battle (2 Samuel
11). It is a shocking abuse of power.

When the prophet Nathan confronts him, David does not make excuses. He confesses
his sin, and Psalm 51 is traditionally linked to this moment. God forgives him,
though the consequences tear through his family for years. David's story shows
both how far a person of faith can fall and how deep God's mercy goes.

## Solomon and the temple

David's son Solomon becomes king around 970 BC. Early in his reign, he asks God
for wisdom to rule well, and God gives it (1 Kings 3). Solomon is known for his
wisdom, and Proverbs and other [[wisdom-literature|wisdom books]] are linked to
him.

Solomon builds the [[temple]] in Jerusalem, a permanent house for God that
replaces the portable tabernacle (1 Kings 6–8). When the ark is brought in, God's
glory fills the temple like a cloud, just as it filled the tabernacle in the
wilderness (1 Kings 8:10-11). For Israel, this is a high point: a king from David's
line, peace in the land, and God dwelling among his people.

## The kingdom breaks apart

Sadly, Solomon's heart drifts. He builds a vast fortune, uses forced labour, and
marries hundreds of wives, many from other nations, whose gods he begins to
worship (1 Kings 11:1-8). These were exactly the dangers Deuteronomy had warned
kings about.

After Solomon dies, around 930 BC, his son Rehoboam treats the people harshly.
The ten northern tribes break away and form their own kingdom, called
[[israel|Israel]], under a man named Jeroboam. Only the southern tribes stay loyal
to David's family. Their kingdom is called Judah, with Jerusalem as its capital (1
Kings 12).

## What comes next

The age of the kings shows both the hope and the heartbreak of Israel's story.
God's promise to David stands firm, but no king in David's line seems able to
live up to it. In the next lesson we will hear from the prophets, who warned both
kingdoms to return to God, and we will follow the people into exile and back.
