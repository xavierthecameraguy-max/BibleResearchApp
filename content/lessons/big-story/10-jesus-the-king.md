Last time we saw Israel's long story reach a painful point. The kingdoms fell,
the temple was destroyed, and the people went into exile. Some returned and
rebuilt, but the great promises of the prophets, a new covenant and a king from
David's line, were still waiting. For about four hundred years, God's people
waited and hoped.

Now, at last, the story turns.

## A baby in Bethlehem

The New Testament opens with four books called Gospels: Matthew, Mark, Luke and
John. The word [[gospel]] means "good news". Each tells the story of Jesus from a
slightly different angle, like four friends describing the same remarkable
person.

Matthew and Luke tell of Jesus' birth. An angel tells a young woman named Mary
that she will have a son by the power of the Holy Spirit, and that he will be
given the throne of his ancestor David (Luke 1:30-33). Mary is engaged to Joseph,
a man from David's family. Jesus is born in Bethlehem, David's hometown, in very
humble conditions, and his first visitors are shepherds.

Christians call this the [[incarnation]], meaning "becoming flesh". They believe
that in Jesus, God's eternal Son became fully human while remaining fully God
(John 1:14). The God who walked in the garden, who lived in the tabernacle and
temple, has now come to live among his people in person.

When was this? Our calendar was designed to count from Jesus' birth, but it was
worked out centuries later and is a few years off. Matthew says Jesus was born
while Herod the Great was king, and Herod died around 4 BC. So most scholars
place Jesus' birth around 6–4 BC.

## The kingdom has come

Around age thirty, Jesus begins his public work. He is baptised in the Jordan
River by his relative John the Baptist, and the Spirit comes on him while a voice
from heaven calls him God's beloved Son (Mark 1:9-11). Then he is led into the
wilderness for forty days, where he is tested but stays faithful.

Then Jesus starts preaching. His message is short and electric: the time has
come, the [[kingdom-of-god|kingdom of God]] has come near, so turn around and
trust the good news (Mark 1:15).

What does "the kingdom of God" mean? It is not a place on a map. It means God's
rule, God setting things right, breaking into the world. For centuries Israel
had prayed for God to come as king. Jesus announces that it is happening, now,
in him.

The call to "turn around" is [[repentance]]: changing direction, away from our
own way and toward God. And "believe" means to trust, to put your weight on this
good news.

## Calling disciples

Jesus does not work alone. Walking by the Sea of Galilee, he calls some fishermen,
Simon Peter, Andrew, James and John, to follow him, saying he will make them
fishers of people (Mark 1:16-20). They leave their nets and go.

Eventually Jesus chooses twelve men to be his closest followers. The number is no
accident. Israel had twelve tribes, so Jesus is signalling that he is rebuilding
God's people around himself. These followers are called [[disciple|disciples]],
which means students or apprentices. They learn not just Jesus' ideas but his
way of life. Women also followed Jesus and supported his work (Luke 8:1-3).

## Signs of the kingdom

The Gospels record many miracles. Jesus heals the sick, gives sight to the
blind, drives out evil spirits, calms a storm and even raises the dead. These are
not just displays of power. They are signs, previews of what the kingdom looks
like. When God is king, sickness, evil and death are pushed back. Creation is
being healed.

Jesus also welcomes people others reject. He eats with tax collectors and
sinners, touches people with skin diseases and treats women and children with
dignity. Religious leaders are often scandalised, but Jesus says he came for the
sick, not the healthy (Mark 2:17).

## Teaching in stories

Jesus often teaches with a [[parable]], a short story from everyday life with a
surprising twist. The kingdom is like a tiny mustard seed that grows into a large
plant. It is like a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to search for one that
is lost. In the parable of the lost son, a father runs to embrace his runaway
child (Luke 15:11-32). These stories invite listeners to see God differently and
to decide how they will respond.

In the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), Jesus teaches on a mountain, a bit like
Moses at Sinai. He blesses the poor in spirit, the grieving and the peacemakers,
and he calls his followers to love even their enemies.

## Israel's story, fulfilled

If you have followed this track, you may notice how much of Jesus' life echoes
the earlier story. He is the son of Abraham and son of David (Matthew 1:1). Like
Israel, he goes down to Egypt as a child and comes back (Matthew 2:13-15). He passes
through water and is tested in the wilderness, faithful where Israel failed. He
chooses twelve, teaches from a mountain, and feeds crowds with bread in the
wilderness, recalling the manna.

The Gospels present Jesus as the [[messiah|Messiah]], the anointed King Israel
had been waiting for. But he is a surprising kind of king. He does not raise an
army or overthrow Rome. When Peter declares that Jesus is the Messiah, Jesus
begins to explain that he must suffer, die and rise again (Mark 8:29-31). The
disciples find this almost impossible to understand.

## What comes next

Jesus' kingdom will not come through a sword. It will come through a cross. In
the next lesson we will follow Jesus to Jerusalem for one final Passover, to his
death and his astonishing resurrection, and then watch the good news spread
across the world.
