Welcome to the story of the church. Over these twelve lessons you will travel two thousand years, from a room full of frightened friends in Jerusalem to a family of believers on every continent. This is your family history. Every Christian you meet today is connected, through a long chain of people, to the events in this first lesson.

## A small beginning

After Jesus rose from the dead, he appeared to his followers for about forty days. Then he gave them a job: to be his witnesses "to the ends of the earth" (Acts 1:8). He told them to wait in Jerusalem for the Holy Spirit.

They did not wait long. At the Jewish festival of [[pentecost]], about fifty days after [[passover]], the Spirit came on them in a dramatic way (Acts 2). Peter, who only weeks before had denied knowing Jesus, stood up and boldly told the crowd that Jesus was the promised [[messiah]], crucified and now raised. About three thousand people were baptised that day. Many Christians call Pentecost the birthday of the [[church]].

Read the key passage, Acts 2:37-47. Notice what the first believers did: they learned from the apostles, shared meals, prayed together, and shared their belongings so nobody went without. That simple picture has inspired Christians ever since.

## A Jewish movement

It is easy to forget that the first Christians were almost all Jewish. Jesus was Jewish, his twelve [[apostle|apostles]] were Jewish, and the first church met in and around the [[temple]] in Jerusalem. They did not think they were starting a new religion. They believed the God of Israel had kept his promises by sending Jesus.

The early church was led by the apostles, especially Peter and John, and later by James, the brother of Jesus. They faced opposition from some religious leaders. A young man named Stephen became the first Christian killed for his faith (Acts 7). After his death, many believers scattered from Jerusalem and took the message with them.

## The door opens to the Gentiles

One of the biggest surprises in Acts is that the good news was not only for Jewish people. A [[gentile]] is anyone who is not Jewish. In Acts 10, Peter visits the home of a Roman army officer named Cornelius, and the Holy Spirit comes on these Gentiles too. Peter realises that God "does not show favouritism".

In the city of Antioch in Syria, Jews and Gentiles began worshipping together. It was there that believers were first called "Christians" (Acts 11:26).

## Paul on the road

The man who did the most to carry the message to the Gentiles was Paul. He had once persecuted Christians, but the risen Jesus met him on the road to Damascus and turned his life around (Acts 9).

From about the late 40s to the early 60s AD, Paul made several long journeys around what is now Turkey and Greece. He usually began in the local synagogue, then taught anyone who would listen. He planted small churches in cities like Philippi, Corinth and Ephesus, and he kept in touch by writing letters. Many of those letters are now in your New Testament, which makes them some of the earliest Christian writings we have.

## The first big disagreement

As more Gentiles joined, a hard question came up. Did Gentile believers need to become Jewish first, including being circumcised and keeping the whole Jewish law?

Around AD 49, the apostles and elders met in Jerusalem to talk it through. This meeting, recorded in Acts 15, is often called the first church [[council]]. After honest debate, they agreed that Gentiles are saved by God's [[grace]] through [[faith]] in Jesus, just as Jewish believers are. They asked Gentile Christians to avoid a few practices that would make fellowship with Jewish believers difficult, but they did not require them to keep the whole law.

This was a turning point. It meant the church could become a worldwide family, not only a group within one nation. It also showed a healthy pattern: when Christians disagree, they can gather, listen to Scripture and to one another, pray, and seek the Spirit's guidance together.

## The fall of the temple

In AD 66, many Jews in Judea rebelled against Roman rule. Rome responded with overwhelming force. In AD 70, Roman armies captured Jerusalem and destroyed the temple. It was a devastating moment for the Jewish people, and the temple has never been rebuilt.

For the church, the destruction of the temple was also a turning point. The Jerusalem church lost its central place, and the movement's centre of gravity shifted to cities like Antioch, Alexandria and Rome. Over the following decades, Christianity and the Judaism of the rabbis grew into two increasingly separate communities, though historians stress that this "parting of the ways" was gradual and happened at different speeds in different places.

## The end of the apostolic age

By the end of the first century, the apostles had died. Christian tradition says that Peter and Paul were killed in Rome in the 60s, and that John lived to old age, dying around AD 100. Historians call this first period the "apostolic age".

What did the apostles leave behind? Churches scattered across the Roman Empire, leaders appointed to care for them, and the writings that would become the New Testament. The baton had passed to a new generation.

## Where you fit in

The church began with ordinary people who were filled with the Spirit and could not stop talking about Jesus. They made mistakes, argued, and faced danger, yet the message kept spreading. When you join a church today, you are stepping into the same story that began at Pentecost.
