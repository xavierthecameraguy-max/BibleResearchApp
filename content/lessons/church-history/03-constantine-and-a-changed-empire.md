In the last lesson, the church faced its fiercest persecution under Diocletian. Within just a few years, the situation turned completely around. An emperor began to support the church, and within a lifetime Christianity went from being a hunted minority to the faith of the empire.

This is one of the biggest turning points in all of church history. Christians have been debating ever since whether it was a blessing, a danger, or both.

## A vision before battle

In the early 300s the Roman Empire had several rival rulers. One of them, Constantine, marched on Rome in AD 312 to fight his rival Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge, just outside the city.

Two early Christian writers describe what happened, and their accounts differ in detail. One says Constantine was told in a dream to mark his soldiers' shields with a symbol of Christ. Another, written much later, says he saw a sign of the cross in the sky with the message that he would conquer by it. Historians do not agree on exactly what happened. What is clear is that Constantine won the battle and credited the Christian God with his victory.

## The Edict of Milan

In AD 313, Constantine and his co-emperor Licinius agreed on a policy usually called the Edict of Milan. It gave Christians, and people of other faiths, freedom to worship. It also ordered that church buildings and property seized during the persecution be given back.

For believers who had lived through the Great Persecution, this must have felt like a miracle. Bishops who had been in prison or forced labour were now honoured guests at the emperor's table.

## What changed under Constantine

Constantine did far more than tolerate Christians. He gave money to build grand churches, including early versions of St Peter's in Rome and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. He gave [[bishop|bishops]] legal powers and privileges. He made Sunday a public day of rest. In 325 he called the first great [[council]] of the church at Nicaea, which you will meet in the next lesson.

Around 330 he moved his capital east to the old city of Byzantium, renamed Constantinople (modern Istanbul). This "new Rome" would become the heart of Eastern Christianity for over a thousand years.

## Was Constantine really a Christian?

This is one of history's great debates, and you should know that historians genuinely disagree.

Some point out that Constantine kept certain titles and symbols from the old religion, used coins with the sun god for years, and had members of his own family executed. He was [[baptism|baptised]] only on his deathbed in 337. That was a fairly common practice at the time, because many believed baptism washed away sins and wanted to receive it as late as possible.

Others argue that his support for the church was costly and sincere, that he spoke and wrote about his faith, and that the slow pace of change was a wise way to rule an empire that was still mostly pagan.

Many historians land somewhere in between: Constantine was probably a genuine but imperfect believer, who also saw the church as a force that could unite his empire. The [[orthodox|Eastern Orthodox]] Church honours him as a saint. Western churches generally do not, though they recognise his importance.

## Christianity becomes the official religion

After Constantine, most emperors were Christian, though one, Julian, tried to bring back the old gods in the 360s. In AD 380, the emperor Theodosius I issued a decree declaring that the faith confessed at Nicaea was to be the religion of all his subjects. Over the following years, pagan sacrifices were banned and many temples were closed.

So in less than seventy years the church went from persecuted to protected to official. The roles had reversed, and sadly, Christians sometimes used their new power to pressure others.

## Blessings and dangers

The new situation brought real blessings. Christians could worship openly, build churches, care for the poor on a large scale, and found hospitals. Bishops could speak to rulers about justice. The faith spread rapidly through the empire.

But there were dangers too. When being a Christian helped your career, many joined for the wrong reasons, and the church grew faster than it could teach its new members. Emperors began to take sides in church arguments and to treat bishops as officials. The line between following Christ and serving the state became blurred. Over the centuries, some Christians would use the state's power to punish those who disagreed with them, which most Christians today see as a betrayal of Jesus' way.

Not everyone was comfortable with these changes. As you will see in lesson six, some believers went out into the deserts to live simply, partly as a protest against a church growing too comfortable.

## Giving to God what is God's

The key passage, Matthew 22:15-22, records Jesus being asked about paying taxes to Caesar. He answered that people should give Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and God what belongs to God. Christians have argued ever since about exactly where that line falls.

Constantine's story invites a question every believer still faces: how do we live as good citizens without letting any government, party or nation take the place that belongs to Jesus alone?
