When people hear "the Middle Ages", they sometimes picture a gloomy time of ignorance between the glory of Rome and the brilliance of the Renaissance. Historians today rarely see it that way. This was also an age of courageous missionaries, beautiful art, careful scholarship and deep prayer. Much of it centred on a surprising group of people: monks.

## Into the desert

Even before Constantine, a few Christians had gone out into the deserts of Egypt and Syria to seek God in simplicity and prayer. As the church became safe and comfortable after him, many more followed, partly because they worried it was losing its edge.

The most famous was Antony of Egypt (about 251–356). As a young man he heard Jesus' words to the rich young man read in church: sell what you have and give to the poor (Matthew 19:21). He did exactly that and spent most of his long life in the desert, praying, fasting and fighting temptation. Athanasius wrote a biography of him that became a bestseller of the ancient world and inspired thousands to follow.

This way of life is called [[monasticism]]. Monks (men) and nuns (women) commit themselves to prayer, simplicity and community. Some lived alone as hermits, but most lived together in communities called monasteries.

## Benedict and his Rule

In the West, the most influential guide to monastic life was written by Benedict of Nursia in Italy, around AD 530. His Rule is short and practical. It organises each day around set times of prayer, manual work and reading. It calls for humility, hospitality and stability, which means staying in one community for life. It is known for its gentleness and balance.

Benedictine monasteries spread across Europe. They farmed land, welcomed travellers, cared for the sick and poor, and ran schools. Monks and nuns still follow Benedict's Rule today.

## Keeping learning alive

In the 400s, the western half of the Roman Empire broke apart under pressure from Germanic peoples. Cities shrank and schools closed. In this turmoil, monasteries became islands of learning.

Before printing, every book had to be copied by hand. Monks spent countless hours in rooms called scriptoria copying the Bible, prayer books and the writings of the church fathers, as well as many works by ancient Greek and Roman authors. Many beautifully decorated [[manuscript|manuscripts]] survive from this period, such as the Book of Kells from Ireland and the Lindisfarne Gospels from northern England. Without these patient copyists, much of the ancient world's writing would have been lost.

## Patrick and the Celtic missions

Patrick was born in Roman Britain, probably in the late 300s or early 400s; his exact dates are uncertain. As a teenager he was kidnapped by raiders and taken to Ireland as a slave. There, in loneliness, he began to pray. After about six years he escaped and returned home. Later he felt God calling him back to the very people who had enslaved him. He spent the rest of his life in Ireland as a missionary bishop, and his own short writings still survive.

Ireland then became a sending country. Irish monks such as Columba, who founded a monastery on the island of Iona around 563, carried the faith to Scotland and northern England. Others travelled across Europe.

## The Anglo-Saxons

Meanwhile, in 597, Pope Gregory the Great sent a monk named Augustine (a different Augustine from the North African one) to the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of southern England. He became the first Archbishop of Canterbury. Celtic and Roman missionaries sometimes clashed over customs, such as how to date Easter, but together they won England to the faith. English missionaries like Boniface later took the [[gospel]] to the Germanic peoples on the continent.

## Cyril and Methodius

In the East, two brothers from Thessalonica, Cyril and Methodius, went as missionaries to the Slavic peoples of central Europe in the 860s. They believed people should hear God's word in their own language. So they developed an alphabet for the Slavonic language and translated Scripture and the [[liturgy]], the church's pattern of worship. A later alphabet, named Cyrillic in Cyril's honour, is still used for Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian and other languages. Their work laid the foundations of Christianity in much of Eastern Europe.

## The rise of the papacy

As imperial power faded in the West, the Bishop of Rome became the most stable authority in the region. Leaders like Leo the Great (440–461) and Gregory the Great (590–604) fed the hungry, negotiated with invaders and sent missionaries.

Catholics see the [[pope]] as the successor of Peter, given a special role by Christ to lead and unite the whole church. Orthodox Christians honoured Rome as the first among the great churches, but did not accept that its bishop had authority over all the others. This difference grew over the centuries, as you will see in the next lesson.

## Charlemagne

On Christmas Day in the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne, king of the Franks, as emperor in Rome. Charlemagne ruled much of western Europe. He promoted schools, better copies of the Bible and church reform. But he also forced some conquered peoples, such as the Saxons, to be baptised at sword point, which most Christians today would see as contrary to the gospel. The coronation also offended the court in Constantinople, which saw its own ruler, at that time the empress Irene, as the true Roman emperor.

## Beautiful feet

The key passage, Romans 10:11-17, asks how people can believe if no one tells them, and praises the beautiful feet of those who bring good news. Most people reading this lesson owe their faith, somewhere far back, to an unnamed monk or missionary who carried the message to a new people. The faith reached you because someone went.
