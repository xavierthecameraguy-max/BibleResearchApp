Jesus warned his followers that they would face hostility because of him (John 15:20). Within a generation, that warning came true. For roughly two and a half centuries, being a Christian in the Roman Empire could cost you your job, your home, or your life.

This lesson tells some of their stories. They are not easy to read, but they matter. Many believers around the world still face danger today, and the early martyrs remind every Christian what faithfulness can look like.

## Why did Rome persecute Christians?

The Roman Empire was usually quite tolerant of different religions. You could worship almost any god you liked, as long as you also honoured the gods of Rome and showed respect to the emperor, who in many places was honoured as divine.

Christians would not do this. They believed there is only one God, and that offering incense to the emperor's image meant worshipping someone other than him. To Romans, this looked like stubbornness and disloyalty. Many believed the gods protected the empire, so when a plague, famine or earthquake struck, people sometimes blamed the Christians for angering the gods.

Rumours made things worse. Christians met privately, spoke of eating "the body and blood" of Jesus, and called each other "brother" and "sister". Outsiders twisted this into accusations of cannibalism and immorality. Christians were even called "atheists" because they rejected the gods.

## Nero: the first great persecution

In AD 64, a huge fire destroyed much of Rome. Rumours spread that the emperor Nero had started it. According to the Roman historian Tacitus, Nero shifted the blame onto the Christians and had many of them cruelly executed. Christian tradition holds that Peter and Paul were killed in Rome around this time.

## Polycarp and Perpetua

Two stories from the second and third centuries became especially loved.

Polycarp was the elderly bishop of Smyrna (in modern Turkey). A [[bishop]] was a senior church leader who cared for the believers of a city. Around AD 155 he was arrested and told to curse Christ to save his life. According to the account written soon after, he replied that he had served Christ for eighty-six years and Christ had never wronged him, so how could he betray his King? He was put to death.

In AD 203, in Carthage in North Africa, a young noblewoman named Perpetua was arrested with several companions, including an enslaved woman named Felicity. Perpetua was a new believer and a nursing mother. Her father begged her to give up her faith, but she refused. Part of her prison diary survives, making it one of the earliest writings we have by a Christian woman. She and her friends were killed in the arena.

## Waves, not a constant flood

It would be easy to imagine that Christians spent three centuries hiding in catacombs. Historians today paint a more mixed picture. For long stretches, persecution was sporadic and local. It depended on a hostile governor, an angry crowd, or a neighbour with a grudge. In many places and decades, Christians lived fairly openly, owned property and even built meeting places.

Then came a few empire-wide campaigns.

In AD 250, the emperor Decius ordered everyone to offer a [[sacrifice]] to the gods and get a certificate proving they had done it. Many Christians refused and died. Others gave in, or bought fake certificates. Afterwards the church had a painful debate: should those who gave in be allowed back? Most churches decided that, after real [[repentance]], they could be welcomed home.

A few years later, in 257–258, the emperor Valerian targeted church leaders in particular. Among those executed was Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage.

The worst came last. From AD 303 to about 311, under the emperor Diocletian and his co-rulers, the "Great Persecution" tried to wipe Christianity out. Churches were torn down, Scriptures were burned, and many believers were imprisoned, tortured or killed, especially in the eastern half of the empire.

## Witnesses

The word [[martyr]] comes from the Greek word for "witness". The early church saw the martyrs as people whose deaths bore witness to Jesus. Believers remembered them each year on the day they died, which is the origin of many saints' days still kept today.

Around AD 197, the North African writer Tertullian made a famous point to the Roman authorities. In paraphrase: the more you cut us down, the more of us there are, because the blood of Christians is like seed. People were struck by martyrs who died with calm courage, forgiving their killers. Many wanted to know what gave them such hope.

## Why this matters for you

The early church did not grow because it was powerful. It grew while it was weak, through ordinary believers who loved their neighbours, cared for the sick during plagues, and stayed faithful under pressure.

The key passage, 1 Peter 4:12-19, was written to Christians facing suffering. It tells them not to be surprised, to keep doing good, and to entrust themselves to a faithful Creator. Most readers of this lesson will never face a lion in an arena. But everyone faces moments when following Jesus costs something. The martyrs show that God's people can stand firm, and that he does not abandon them.
