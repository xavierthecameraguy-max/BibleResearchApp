If you drive through almost any town, you may pass a Baptist church, a Catholic parish, a Methodist chapel and a Pentecostal fellowship, all within a few streets. For a new believer, this can be bewildering. Are they all Christian? Which one is right? Why can't they just agree?

The past few lessons have explained how many of these groups came about. This lesson draws the map together.

## Three great families

Most of the world's Christians belong to one of three large families.

**The Orthodox churches.** The [[orthodox|Eastern Orthodox]] churches, such as the Greek, Russian, Romanian and Antiochian, trace their roots to the eastern Roman Empire. They see themselves as the continuation of the undivided early church. Worship is rich with icons, chant and ancient [[liturgy]]. The Oriental Orthodox churches, such as the Coptic and Ethiopian, form a related but separate family (see lesson five).

**The Catholic Church.** The [[catholic|Roman Catholic Church]] is the largest Christian body, with around 1.4 billion members. It is united under the [[pope]], whom Catholics see as Peter's successor. It also includes Eastern Catholic churches, which keep Eastern forms of worship while in communion with Rome.

**The Protestant churches.** [[protestant|Protestant]] churches trace their roots to the 16th-century Reformation or to later movements. They share an emphasis on the authority of Scripture and salvation by grace through faith. Within this family there are many branches.

## The main Protestant branches

- **Lutheran.** Following Martin Luther, strong in Germany, Scandinavia and parts of the United States and Africa. Lutherans stress justification by faith and Christ's real presence in communion.
- **Reformed and Presbyterian.** From Calvin, Knox and others. They stress God's sovereignty and are usually governed by elders ("presbyters").
- **Anglican and Episcopal.** From the Church of England, now a worldwide communion, with many members in Africa. Anglicans combine Reformation teaching with bishops and set prayers.
- **Baptist.** From the early 1600s in England and the Netherlands. Baptists baptise believers rather than infants and usually let each congregation govern itself.
- **Methodist.** From the 18th-century revival led by John and Charles Wesley (see lesson eleven). Methodists stress holy living, God's grace offered to all, and social care.
- **Pentecostal and charismatic.** From the early 20th century. They stress a powerful experience of the Holy Spirit and gifts like healing and speaking in tongues. This is now one of the fastest-growing movements worldwide, and charismatic believers are also found in many other denominations.
- **Non-denominational.** Many churches today are independent, not part of any [[denomination]]. Most still draw on one or more of the streams above, often Baptist or charismatic.

## What they share

It is easy to focus on differences, but the common ground is enormous. Nearly all of these churches confess:

- One God in three persons, the [[trinity]].
- Jesus Christ, fully God and fully human, who died for our sins and rose bodily from the dead.
- The Bible as God's inspired word.
- Salvation as God's gift of [[grace]].
- The call to love God and neighbour, and the hope of Christ's return and the [[new-creation]].

Most use or affirm the Nicene Creed, and many the Apostles' Creed. All of these churches are seeking to follow the same Lord.

## What they differ on

Here are some of the most common differences, explained as each side would put it.

**Baptism.** Orthodox, Catholics, Lutherans, Anglicans, Presbyterians and Methodists baptise infants, seeing [[baptism]] as God's gracious act that welcomes a child into the covenant community, like circumcision in the Old Testament. Baptists, Pentecostals and many others baptise only those who can profess faith, seeing baptism as a believer's public response to God's grace.

**Church government.** Some churches are led by bishops (called "episcopal" government). Others are led by elders, regionally and locally (Presbyterian). Others let each congregation make its own decisions (congregational). Each side points to New Testament patterns to support its view.

**The Lord's Supper.** Orthodox and Catholics believe the bread and wine truly become Christ's body and blood. Lutherans believe Christ is truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine. Reformed Christians speak of a real spiritual presence received by faith. Many Baptists and others see it mainly as a memorial meal. All agree it is central to Christian worship.

**Spiritual gifts.** Pentecostals and charismatics believe gifts such as tongues and prophecy are for today and should be eagerly sought. Some other Protestants, sometimes called cessationists, believe those particular gifts were mainly for the apostolic age. Many are somewhere in between.

**Authority.** Catholics and Orthodox see Scripture and Tradition as working together, guarded by the church. Protestants hold to [[sola-scriptura]], with the Bible as the final authority.

## A gracious approach

How should you handle all this? Here are a few suggestions.

First, be humble. These questions have been debated by wise and godly people for centuries. Second, learn what your own church believes and why, but also try to understand others as they understand themselves. Third, focus on the centre: Jesus Christ and him crucified and risen. Fourth, love those who disagree with you. A saying often used, though its origin is uncertain, puts it well: in essentials unity, in non-essentials liberty, in all things love.

The key passage, 1 Corinthians 12:12-27, pictures the church as one body with many parts. Differences between churches are not the same as differences between body parts, and many are real disagreements that matter. But Paul's picture reminds us that we need each other, and that Christ is the head of us all.
