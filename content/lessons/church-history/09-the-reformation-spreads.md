Once Luther had opened the door, others rushed through it, and not all in the same direction. Within a generation, Western Christianity had divided into several streams. At the same time, the Catholic Church was going through a great renewal of its own. This lesson tells both stories, including some painful chapters.

## Zwingli in Zurich

In Zurich, Switzerland, a priest named Ulrich Zwingli began preaching straight through the Gospel of Matthew in 1519. He soon called for the city to remove images from churches, end compulsory fasting and let clergy marry.

Zwingli and Luther agreed on much, but in 1529 they met at Marburg and could not agree about the Lord's Supper. Luther insisted that Christ's body and blood are truly present in the bread and wine. Zwingli saw the meal mainly as a memorial. It was an early sign that Protestants would not be one single movement. Zwingli was killed in battle in 1531.

## Calvin in Geneva

The most influential second-generation reformer was John Calvin, a French scholar who first worked in Geneva from 1536 until the city expelled him in 1538. After three years in Strasbourg, he returned, and from 1541 until his death in 1564 he preached, taught and organised the church there. His book *Institutes of the Christian Religion* became one of the most important works of Protestant theology.

Calvin stressed God's sovereignty, the glory of God in all of life, and the authority of Scripture. He also developed a pattern of church government by elders. Geneva trained pastors who carried these ideas to France, the Netherlands, Scotland, Hungary and beyond. The churches that came from this stream are called Reformed, and in Scotland, led by John Knox, Presbyterian.

## The Anabaptists

Some reformers felt that Luther and Zwingli had not gone far enough. In Zurich in 1525, a small group baptised each other as adults, believing [[baptism]] should follow personal [[faith]]. Their opponents called them [[anabaptist|Anabaptists]], meaning "re-baptisers".

Many Anabaptists believed the church should be a community of committed disciples, separate from the state, and that Christians should not use violence or take oaths. Both Catholic and Protestant authorities saw them as dangerous, and thousands were executed. Their heirs include the Mennonites, named after Menno Simons, as well as the Amish and Hutterites. Baptists later developed believers' baptism in their own way, in the early 1600s.

## The English Reformation

England's story was different. King Henry VIII wanted his marriage to Catherine of Aragon annulled because he had no male heir. When the pope refused, Henry broke with Rome, and in 1534 Parliament declared him head of the Church of England. Henry himself remained fairly Catholic in belief, but he closed the monasteries and allowed an English Bible in churches.

Under his son Edward VI, the church became more Protestant, and Thomas Cranmer wrote the *Book of Common Prayer*. Under Mary I it returned to Rome, and around 280 Protestants were burned. Under Elizabeth I, from 1558, a settlement was reached that combined Protestant teaching with much traditional worship and church order, including [[bishop|bishops]]. Catholics, in turn, suffered fines, imprisonment and in some cases execution. This English tradition became [[anglican|Anglicanism]]. In the United States its main branch is called the Episcopal Church.

## The Catholic Reformation

It would be a mistake to think the Catholic Church simply reacted to the Protestants. Many Catholics had been working for renewal before Luther, and that work now gathered pace. Historians speak of both a Catholic Reformation (renewal from within) and a Counter-Reformation (response to Protestantism).

The centrepiece was the Council of Trent, which met in the city of Trent, in what is now northern Italy, in three periods between 1545 and 1563. It clearly defined Catholic teaching: that Scripture and Tradition together hand on God's revelation, that justification is by grace and involves real inner renewal, that there are seven [[sacrament|sacraments]], and that the [[deuterocanonical]] books, such as Tobit and 1 and 2 Maccabees, belong in the Bible. It also tackled abuses, ordering seminaries to train priests and requiring bishops to live among their people.

## The Jesuits

New religious orders brought fresh energy. The most famous was the Society of Jesus, or Jesuits, founded by Ignatius of Loyola, a Spanish soldier who turned to God while recovering from a battle wound. His *Spiritual Exercises*, a month-long guide to prayer and discernment, are still widely used. Approved in 1540, the Jesuits became famous for their schools and universities and for missionaries like Francis Xavier, who travelled to India and Japan.

Other reformers included Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross, whose writings on prayer are treasured by Christians of many traditions.

## The tragedy of the wars

The darkest side of this era was violence. Rulers often decided the religion of their lands, and religion became tangled with politics and power. France suffered decades of civil war, including the massacre of thousands of Protestants on St Bartholomew's Day in 1572. The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) devastated much of central Europe. Historians note that these wars had political causes too, but religion fuelled the hatred.

The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 ended the Thirty Years' War and helped close the era of religious wars. Slowly, many Christians began to see that faith cannot be forced.

## One body

The key passage, Ephesians 4:1-16, calls believers to keep the unity of the Spirit, because there is one body, one Lord, one faith and one baptism. Christians of all traditions today look back with sorrow at the times they treated each other as enemies, and many are working to understand one another better.
