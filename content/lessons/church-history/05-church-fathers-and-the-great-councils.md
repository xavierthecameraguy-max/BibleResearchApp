After the apostles died, who explained the faith to the next generations? Much of that work fell to a remarkable group of pastors, bishops and scholars. Christians call them the [[church-fathers|church fathers]]. They were not perfect, and they did not always agree, but their writings are a window into how the early church read the Bible, worshipped and lived.

## Meet a few of the fathers

**Ignatius of Antioch** (died about 110) was a bishop who wrote seven short letters while being taken under guard to Rome to be executed. They show a church that already valued its [[bishop|bishops]], celebrated the Lord's Supper, and treasured unity.

**Irenaeus of Lyon** (about 130–202) grew up in Asia Minor, where as a boy he heard Polycarp preach. He later led the church in Lyon, in Gaul (modern France). He wrote a major work against teachers called Gnostics, who claimed secret knowledge and taught that the physical world was evil. Irenaeus insisted that the God who made the world is the same God who saves it.

**The Cappadocian Fathers** were three friends from Cappadocia (central Turkey) in the late 300s: Basil the Great, his brother Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus. Their careful thinking helped the church explain the [[trinity]] at the Council of Constantinople in 381. Basil also founded a large centre for caring for the poor and sick.

**John Chrysostom** (about 347–407) was a preacher in Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople. His nickname means "golden-mouthed". He preached verse by verse through books of the Bible and spoke boldly against the wealthy who ignored the poor, which got him into trouble with the empress.

**Augustine of Hippo** (354–430) lived in North Africa. As a young man he chased success and pleasure, but after years of searching he became a Christian in 386. His book *Confessions* tells that story as a long prayer to God. Augustine wrote about [[grace]], [[sin]], the church and history, and he has influenced Western Christianity, both Catholic and Protestant, more than almost anyone except the apostles.

Orthodox, Catholic and many Protestant Christians all read and value the fathers, though they weigh their authority differently. Orthodox and Catholic Christians see them as key witnesses to the church's living Tradition. Protestants often value them as wise guides who must be tested by Scripture.

## The next big question: who is Jesus?

Nicaea had settled that the Son is fully God. But that raised another question. If Jesus is fully God, how is he also truly human? Did he have a real human mind and will? Is he two persons sharing one body, or one person?

In the 400s, two main approaches clashed. Some teachers, linked with the city of Antioch, stressed Jesus' full humanity so strongly that critics feared they were splitting him into two persons. Others, linked with Alexandria, stressed his unity so strongly that critics feared his humanity was being swallowed up by his divinity.

## Chalcedon, 451

In 451 about 500 bishops met at Chalcedon, near Constantinople, for what became the fourth [[ecumenical-council]]. They declared that Jesus Christ is one person who is both fully God and fully human. He has two natures, divine and human, which are not mixed together into something new, not changed, not divided and not separated.

That can sound abstract, but the point is precious. Because Jesus is fully God, he can save us. Because he is fully human, he truly shares our life, our temptations and our death. The [[incarnation]] means God came all the way to us.

## Seven councils

The Orthodox and Catholic churches both accept seven ecumenical councils, from Nicaea in 325 to the second council of Nicaea in 787. The last one approved the use of icons, the painted images of Christ and the saints that are central to Orthodox worship. The Catholic Church recognises further councils held later in the West. Most Protestants accept the teaching of the first four councils about God and Christ as faithful to Scripture, while holding that councils can err and must be tested by the Bible.

## Churches that said no

Not everyone accepted Chalcedon. Many Christians in Egypt, Ethiopia, Armenia and Syria felt that its language of "two natures" leaned too far towards dividing Christ. They preferred an older formula from Cyril of Alexandria about the "one nature of God the Word made flesh". These churches are today called the [[oriental-orthodox|Oriental Orthodox]] churches. They include the Coptic Orthodox Church of Egypt, the Armenian Apostolic Church, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and the Syriac Orthodox Church.

Their opponents sometimes called them "Monophysites", but they reject that label because they do not believe Christ's humanity was lost. Many prefer the term "Miaphysite", meaning "one united nature". They confess that he is fully God and fully human, united without mixture. In recent decades, theologians from these churches, the Eastern Orthodox and the Catholic Church have agreed that they share the same basic faith about Christ, and that much of the old dispute was over words. Historians add that politics and language barriers also played a large part. These ancient churches have kept the faith through centuries of hardship.

## The Church of the East

Further east still, beyond the Roman Empire in Persia, was another ancient community: the Church of the East. It did not accept the Council of Ephesus (431), and outsiders often called it "Nestorian", a label its members dispute. It became one of history's great missionary churches, reaching India, Central Asia and China by the 600s. A stone monument erected in China in 781 records its arrival there. Its heirs today include the Assyrian Church of the East.

## Your takeaway

Read Philippians 2:5-11. Paul describes Christ who was in the form of God yet humbled himself, took on human nature, and died on a cross. The fathers and councils were trying to protect that wonder. When you worship Jesus, you are worshipping someone who is truly God and truly one of us.
