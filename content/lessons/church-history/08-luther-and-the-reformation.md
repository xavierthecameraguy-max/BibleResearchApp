On 31 October 1517, a monk and university teacher in the small German town of Wittenberg published a list of points for debate. He probably had no idea that he was about to change the Western world. His name was Martin Luther, and the movement that followed is called the [[reformation]].

## The late medieval church

By 1500, the church in western Europe was powerful and wealthy. It ran schools, hospitals and universities, and it shaped every part of life from birth to death. There was deep and sincere faith among ordinary people.

But there were also real problems, which many Catholics at the time admitted. Some bishops held several posts at once for the income and rarely visited their people. Some priests were poorly trained. Popes of the period were often more like Italian princes than pastors. Many people, both inside and outside the church, called for reform.

## Forerunners

Luther was not the first to protest. In England in the 1370s, John Wycliffe, an Oxford scholar, criticised church wealth and the pope's power and inspired a [[translation]] of the Bible into English. In Bohemia (now the Czech Republic), Jan Hus preached similar ideas in the early 1400s. He was promised safe conduct to a church council, but was arrested and burned at the stake in 1415. His followers carried on his movement.

## Indulgences

The spark for Luther was [[indulgence|indulgences]]. Catholic teaching held, and still holds, that even after sins are forgiven, there can remain a "temporal punishment", a need for healing and purification, which may continue after death in purgatory. The church taught that it could grant an indulgence, drawing on the merits of Christ and the saints, to release people from some or all of this, usually in connection with prayer, good works or a gift.

In 1517 a preacher named Johann Tetzel was selling indulgences near Wittenberg to help pay for rebuilding St Peter's Basilica in Rome. His sales pitch suggested that money could swiftly free loved ones from purgatory. Luther was alarmed. He feared that people were being taught to trust in a piece of paper rather than in God's mercy, and to skip true [[repentance]].

## The 95 Theses

Luther wrote 95 points, or theses, for debate, challenging the way indulgences were being preached. Tradition says he nailed them to the door of the castle church in Wittenberg. Historians debate whether that actually happened, but he certainly sent them to his archbishop. Within weeks they had been printed and spread across Germany.

## The power of print

About seventy years earlier, Johannes Gutenberg had developed printing with movable type. For the first time, ideas could be copied quickly and cheaply. Luther wrote in lively, punchy German, and his pamphlets sold in enormous numbers. Woodcut pictures carried his message to people who could not read. The Reformation was, among other things, a media revolution.

## Here I stand

As the dispute grew, Luther's views became more radical. He came to believe that popes and councils could err, and that Scripture alone has the final say. In 1520 the pope threatened to excommunicate him, and Luther publicly burned the document.

In 1521 he was summoned before the Holy Roman Emperor, Charles V, at an imperial assembly called the Diet of Worms. Asked to take back his writings, he refused unless he could be shown wrong by Scripture and clear reason. His conscience, he said, was captive to God's word. Tradition adds the words "Here I stand", though scholars are unsure he said them. He was declared an outlaw, but his prince, Frederick the Wise, had him secretly hidden in Wartburg Castle.

## What Luther taught

Luther had long struggled with guilt and fear before God. While studying passages like Romans 1:16-17 and the key passage, Romans 3:21-28, he came to a joyful conviction: God's righteousness is not only a standard we fail to meet, but a gift he gives. Sinners are declared right with God, which is what [[justification]] means, by [[grace]] alone, received through [[faith]] alone, because of Christ alone. Good works matter, but they flow from being saved rather than earning salvation.

He also taught [[sola-scriptura]], "Scripture alone": the Bible is the final authority over church traditions. And he taught the "priesthood of all believers": every Christian has direct access to God through Christ.

## A Bible for the people

While hiding at the Wartburg, Luther translated the New Testament from Greek into German in about eleven weeks. It was published in 1522, and the whole Bible followed in 1534. His vivid translation helped shape the modern German language and inspired translators elsewhere, including William Tyndale in English.

## The Catholic perspective

It is important to understand how Catholics saw all this, then and now. The Catholic Church agreed that there were abuses and later banned the sale of indulgences. But it believed Luther was wrong on key points.

Catholics hold that justification is a work of God's grace from start to finish, but that grace truly transforms a person, and faith works through love. They saw Luther's "faith alone" as risking a faith with no fruit. They also believed that Scripture and the church's living Tradition belong together, and that without a teaching authority, Christians would split into endless factions. In 1999, Lutherans and Catholics signed a joint declaration agreeing on the basics of justification by grace, while recognising remaining differences.

Luther wanted to reform the church, not leave it. Sadly, the result was a lasting division in Western Christianity, as the next lesson shows.
