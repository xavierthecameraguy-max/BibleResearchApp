Imagine a group of friends who have all met the same remarkable person. They agree he was wonderful. But when someone asks, "Who exactly is he?" they discover they mean very different things.

That is roughly what happened to the church in the early 300s. Everyone agreed Jesus was Lord and Saviour. But what did that mean? Was Jesus truly God, or something a little less? The answer shaped Christianity for ever.

## Why this question matters

You might wonder why Christians argued so fiercely over what can sound like a technical point. The early church saw it as a question about salvation itself.

If Jesus is only a very great creature, then in him we meet a messenger from God, not God himself. But the New Testament says that God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself (2 Corinthians 5:19). The church fathers reasoned that only God can save, only God can defeat death, and only God should be worshipped. If Jesus is not fully God, Christians who pray to him and worship him would be making a terrible mistake.

## Arius and his question

Around AD 318, a popular and gifted priest in Alexandria, Egypt, named [[arianism|Arius]] began teaching that the Son of God was not eternal. In his view, God the Father created the Son first, before anything else, and then created the world through him. The Son was glorious and divine in a lesser sense, but not equal to the Father. Arius summed it up with a catchy line: "there was when he was not".

Arius put his ideas into songs, and they spread quickly among sailors, dockworkers and ordinary believers. Soon the eastern church was in uproar.

## The Council of Nicaea, 325

The emperor Constantine wanted a united church, so in 325 he called bishops from across the empire to the town of Nicaea, in modern Turkey. About 250 to 300 bishops came. Some still bore scars from the persecutions. This was the first [[ecumenical-council]], meaning a council meant to represent the whole church.

After intense debate, the council rejected Arius' teaching as [[heresy]]. It produced a short statement of faith declaring that the Son is "true God from true God", "begotten, not made", and of one being with the Father. The key Greek word was *homoousios*, meaning "of the same substance" or "same being". It said that whatever the Father is as God, the Son is too.

## Athanasius against the world

Nicaea did not end the argument. For the next fifty years, emperors and bishops kept switching sides. A young deacon at Nicaea named Athanasius later became bishop of Alexandria and spent his life defending the council's teaching. He was sent into exile five times. People later spoke of "Athanasius against the world" because at times he seemed to stand nearly alone.

His central point was simple: if Jesus is not truly God, he cannot bring us to God. Only the Creator can renew his creation.

## Constantinople, 381, and the Nicene Creed

The debate was finally settled at a second ecumenical council, held in Constantinople in 381. It confirmed Nicaea and expanded its statement, especially about the Holy Spirit, describing the Spirit as "the Lord, the giver of life", who is worshipped and glorified together with the Father and the Son.

The result is the [[creed]] most Christians simply call the Nicene Creed. It is regularly said in Sunday worship in Orthodox, Catholic, Anglican and Lutheran churches, and many other Protestant churches affirm it as a faithful summary of Scripture.

There is one difference you may notice. In the West, the phrase "and the Son" was later added to say that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. This addition, called the [[filioque]], was not accepted by Eastern churches. You will see in lesson seven how it became part of a much larger split. But the creed's main teaching is shared by all.

## The Trinity, simply put

The Nicene Creed expresses what Christians call the [[trinity]]. It is not a maths puzzle, and it is not three gods. It means this: there is one God, and this one God exists eternally as three persons, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Each is fully God. They are distinct from one another, but never separate.

The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible, but the church believed it summed up what Scripture shows: one God (Deuteronomy 6:4), a Father who sends, a Son who becomes human, and a Spirit who gives life, all honoured as God (Matthew 28:19). Christians admit there is mystery here. God is greater than our minds can fully grasp. The creed draws the boundaries, so we know what we are worshipping.

## The Apostles' Creed

You may also hear the Apostles' Creed, especially in Western churches. Despite its name, it was not written by the twelve apostles. Scholars trace it to a short creed used at baptisms in the church at Rome in the second and third centuries, often called the Old Roman Creed. A new believer would be asked, "Do you believe in God the Father?", "Do you believe in Christ Jesus?", "Do you believe in the Holy Spirit?" and would answer before being baptised. Over several centuries it grew into the form used today.

The Apostles' Creed is used mainly by Catholics and Protestants. The Orthodox do not use it in worship, but they hold the same faith through the Nicene Creed.

## Your creed too

Read John 1:1-18, the key passage. John says the Word was with God and was God, and that the Word became flesh. The creeds are an attempt to say that clearly and guard it carefully. When you say the Nicene Creed, you join believers across seventeen centuries and nearly every tradition, saying together who God is.
