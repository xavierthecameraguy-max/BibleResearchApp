The key passage, Matthew 28:16-20, records Jesus sending his followers to make [[disciple|disciples]] of all nations. Christians have always taken that command seriously, but in the last three centuries something remarkable happened. Waves of renewal swept through the churches, and believers set out in greater numbers than ever before to take the good news to every part of the world.

## Pietism and the Moravians

After the wars of religion, many Protestant churches in Germany had become formal and dry. In the late 1600s, a pastor named Philipp Jakob Spener called for a warmer, more personal faith, with small groups for Bible study and prayer. This movement is called Pietism, from the word for devotion.

Pietism shaped a small community of refugees from Moravia (in today's Czech Republic) who settled on the estate of Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf in Germany. In 1727 these Moravians experienced a deep spiritual renewal. They started a prayer meeting that is said to have continued around the clock for about a hundred years. From 1732, they began sending missionaries to the Caribbean, Greenland, North America and Africa, often ordinary craftspeople who supported themselves.

## The Great Awakening

A Moravian group on a ship to America deeply impressed a young Anglican priest named John Wesley. Back in London in 1738, at a meeting on Aldersgate Street, he felt his heart "strangely warmed" as he heard Luther's words about faith in Christ read aloud. He became sure that Christ had taken away his sins.

Wesley, his brother Charles, who wrote thousands of hymns, and their friend George Whitefield began preaching in fields and town squares, often to thousands of people, including miners and factory workers who rarely went to church. Whitefield crossed the Atlantic many times. In New England, the pastor and thinker Jonathan Edwards saw revival in his own church and wrote carefully about what true spiritual experience looks like.

Historians call this wave of revival in the 1730s and 1740s the Great Awakening, and it gave rise to the Methodist movement and a broad stream of [[evangelical]] Christianity. Later waves, sometimes called the Second Great Awakening, followed in the early 1800s.

## William Carey and the modern missions movement

Catholic missionaries had already been carrying the faith worldwide for centuries. Orders like the Franciscans, Dominicans and Jesuits had worked across the Americas, Africa and Asia since the 1500s, with figures like Francis Xavier in Asia and Matteo Ricci in China. But Protestants had been slow to send missionaries.

That changed with William Carey, an English shoemaker and Baptist pastor who taught himself several languages. In 1792 he published a booklet arguing that Christians have a duty to take the gospel to all nations. That same year he helped found the Baptist Missionary Society, and in 1793 he sailed for India. He worked there for over forty years, translating the Bible into several Indian languages and campaigning against social evils. Many call him the father of modern Protestant [[missions]].

Others followed. In 1865, James Hudson Taylor founded the China Inland Mission. He insisted that missionaries dress and live like the Chinese people and go deep into the interior. Countless women also served as missionaries, teachers, doctors and nurses, often doing far more than they were given credit for.

## Faith in action: abolition

Revival also stirred social change. In Britain, William Wilberforce, an evangelical member of Parliament, spent decades campaigning with others to end the slave trade. It was abolished in the British Empire in 1807, and slavery itself in 1833. Former slaves like Olaudah Equiano told their own stories, which moved many hearts. In the United States, many Christians, Black and white, worked to end slavery, including the formerly enslaved Harriet Tubman and Frederick Douglass.

It must be said honestly that other Christians defended slavery and used the Bible to do so. The struggle was fought within the churches as well as outside them.

## Missions and empire: an honest look

The great age of missions was also the age of European empires. This connection is complicated, and historians disagree about how to weigh it.

Some missionaries travelled under the protection of colonial powers, shared their prejudices, or treated local cultures as worthless. Sometimes the gospel was confused with European customs. These failures caused real harm.

But the picture is mixed. Many missionaries learned local languages, wrote them down for the first time, translated the Bible, founded schools and hospitals, and spoke out against colonial abuses. Most importantly, the faith usually spread through local believers: evangelists, catechists and pastors from among the people themselves. When colonial rule ended, Christianity in Africa and Asia often grew faster than before, which suggests it had truly taken root.

## Azusa Street and Pentecostalism

In 1906, in a run-down building on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, an African American preacher named William Seymour led a revival marked by speaking in tongues, healing and joyful worship. Remarkably for the time, Black, white, Latino and Asian worshippers gathered together. Visitors carried the experience around the world.

This was one of the beginnings of the [[pentecostal|Pentecostal]] movement, which takes its name from [[pentecost]]. It is now one of the largest Christian movements on earth, especially in Africa, Latin America and Asia.
