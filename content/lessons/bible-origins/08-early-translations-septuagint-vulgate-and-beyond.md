Imagine trying to follow Jesus without being able to read a single word of the Bible in a language you understand. For much of history, and for many people even today, that has been a real problem. The story of early Bible translations is the story of Christians working hard to solve it.

## A faith that speaks every language

Our key passage describes the day of [[pentecost]], when the Holy Spirit came upon Jesus' followers in Jerusalem. Visitors from all over the known world heard the disciples speaking about God's great deeds, and each heard it in their own native language. The crowd was amazed.

Many Christians see that day as a sign of something deep in the faith. The good news of Jesus is not tied to one language or culture. It can be truly heard in every tongue. Some religions treat their scriptures as properly readable only in the original language. Christianity, from the start, has been a translating faith. A [[translation]] of the Bible is still God's word to those who read it.

## The Septuagint: a Jewish Greek Bible

Translation started even before Jesus. After Alexander the Great's conquests, Greek became the common language of much of the eastern Mediterranean. Large Jewish communities, especially in Alexandria in Egypt, spoke Greek better than Hebrew.

So, from around the 3rd century BC, Jewish scholars began translating their Scriptures into Greek, starting with the Torah. The other books followed over roughly the next two centuries. This translation is called the [[septuagint]], from the Latin for "seventy". The name comes from a story, told in an ancient letter, that about seventy (or seventy-two) translators produced it. Scholars think the story is more legend than history, but the name stuck, and the Septuagint is often written as LXX, the Roman numeral for seventy.

The Septuagint became hugely important for Christians. Many New Testament writers quote the Old Testament in a form close to it. It became the Old Testament of the Greek-speaking church, and it remains the Old Testament of the [[orthodox|Orthodox]] churches today.

## Moving east: Syriac

East of the Roman Empire, in what is now Syria, Iraq and south-east Turkey, many people spoke Syriac, a form of [[aramaic]]. Christianity took root there very early. Around AD 170, a Christian named Tatian combined the four Gospels into a single continuous story in Syriac, known as the Diatessaron.

Over the following centuries, a standard Syriac Bible developed, called the **Peshitta**, which means "simple" or "common". Its origins are debated, with parts probably translated between the 2nd and 5th centuries. The Peshitta is still read in Syriac-speaking churches today, and these churches carried the gospel as far as India and China.

## Moving south: Coptic

In Egypt, most ordinary people spoke Coptic, the last stage of the ancient Egyptian language, written with an alphabet based largely on Greek. From around the 3rd century AD, the Scriptures were translated into several Coptic dialects. This helped the faith spread far beyond the Greek-speaking cities into the villages and the desert, where Egyptian Christians played a large part in the rise of [[monasticism]].

## Moving west: Latin and the Vulgate

In the western half of the Roman Empire, Latin was the main language. By the late 2nd century, there were already Latin translations, now called the Old Latin versions. But they varied widely, and many were rough.

Around AD 382, Pope Damasus asked a brilliant and famously prickly scholar named Jerome to produce a reliable Latin text. Jerome first revised the Gospels. Then, after moving to Bethlehem, he did something unusual for a Christian of his day: he learned Hebrew from Jewish teachers and translated much of the Old Testament directly from the Hebrew, finishing around AD 405.

His work became known as the [[vulgate]], meaning "common" or "popular". It was not welcomed by everyone at first, but over time it became the standard Bible of the Western church for over a thousand years.

## Alphabets for the gospel

Sometimes translators had to invent a way of writing before they could translate at all.

In the 4th century, a missionary bishop named Ulfilas (also spelled Wulfila) worked among the Goths, a Germanic people. He created an alphabet for their language and translated most of the Bible into Gothic.

In the early 5th century, a monk named Mesrop Mashtots developed an alphabet for Armenian, and the Bible was soon translated. Translations also appeared in Georgian and in Ge'ez for the Christians of Ethiopia.

In the 860s, two brothers from Thessalonica, Cyril and Methodius, were sent to the Slavic peoples of central Europe. They created an alphabet, the Glagolitic script, and translated Scripture and worship into the language now called Old Church Slavonic. A later alphabet developed by their followers was named Cyrillic in Cyril's honour, and it is still used for Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian and other languages.

## The story continues

This work has never stopped. Today the whole Bible has been translated into more than 700 languages, with parts of it available in thousands more, and translators are still at work.

When you read the Bible in your own language, you are standing at the end of a long line of faithful people who believed that God wants to speak to everyone, in the words of their own hearts. Just as at Pentecost, each of us gets to hear about God's mighty works in our own tongue.
