If you visit a Catholic friend and open their Bible, you might be surprised to find books you have never heard of, such as Tobit or Sirach. An Orthodox friend's Bible might contain a few more. Is someone adding books? Is someone taking them away?

This lesson explains why the lists differ, and tries to describe each tradition's reasoning in a way its own members would recognise. It is a topic where sincere Christians disagree, so it is worth approaching with patience and respect.

## The numbers

- **Protestant Bibles** have 66 books: 39 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New.
- **Catholic Bibles** have 73 books. The extra seven are Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom (also called the Wisdom of Solomon), Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus) and Baruch. Catholic Bibles also include longer versions of Esther and Daniel.
- **Orthodox Bibles** include all of these and usually a few more, such as 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh and Psalm 151. The exact list varies somewhat between Orthodox churches, for example between Greek and Russian traditions. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church, one of the ancient [[oriental-orthodox|Oriental Orthodox]] churches, has the largest Bible of all, traditionally counted as 81 books, including Enoch and Jubilees.

The key point: the New Testament is the same in all three. Every tradition accepts the same 27 books. The differences are all in the Old Testament.

## Two forms of the Old Testament

To understand why, we need to go back to the time before Jesus. As we saw in lesson 5, many Jews lived outside the land of Israel and spoke Greek. They used the [[septuagint]], a Greek translation of the Scriptures. Copies of the Septuagint, as preserved by Christians, also included a number of other Jewish books, mostly written between about 300 BC and AD 100.

The Hebrew Bible used by Jews, which became the [[masoretic-text]], did not include these extra books.

The early church spoke mostly Greek, so it used the Septuagint widely. Many early Christian writers quoted the extra books with respect. At the same time, some leaders noticed that these books were not in the Hebrew Bible and treated them a little differently. So, from early on, there was a range of views.

## What to call them

Even the names differ, and the names reflect the views:

- Catholics and Orthodox often call them the [[deuterocanonical]] books, meaning "second canon". This does not mean "second-rate". It refers to the fact that their place in the canon was settled later than the others.
- Protestants usually call them the [[apocrypha]], meaning "hidden things". Many Protestants value them as useful historical and devotional reading but do not treat them as Scripture.
- The Orthodox sometimes use a Greek word meaning "worthy to be read".

## Jerome

Around AD 400, Jerome, the scholar who produced the Latin [[vulgate]] translation, learned Hebrew and championed what he called the "Hebrew truth". He thought these extra books were good for reading and building up character, but not for establishing teaching. Even so, they were included in the Vulgate, and his contemporary Augustine of Hippo accepted them fully. Through the Middle Ages, the Western church generally read them as part of the Old Testament.

## The Protestant view

At the [[reformation]] in the 1500s, Protestant leaders looked again at the question. Martin Luther's German Bible of 1534 placed these books in a separate section between the Old and New Testaments. He described them as useful and good to read, but not equal to the rest of Scripture. The Church of England took a similar view, reading them for examples of life but not to establish teaching.

Protestants give several reasons. These books were not in the Hebrew Bible of the Jews, to whom, Paul says, the words of God were entrusted (Romans 3:2). Jesus and the apostles never quote them directly as Scripture. Jerome himself had doubts. And some Protestants noted that a few teachings, such as prayer for the dead in 2 Maccabees 12, did not seem to fit with the rest of Scripture as they read it. In later centuries, many Protestant publishers left these books out altogether.

## The Catholic view

The Catholic Church responded at the Council of Trent in 1546, which formally declared these books to be fully part of the Old Testament. Catholics see this as confirming, not inventing, what the church had long done. The Council of Florence had already listed the same books in 1442.

Catholics point out that the early church used the Septuagint, which included these books, and that regional councils such as Hippo and Carthage around AD 400 listed them. They note that the New Testament writers often quoted the Septuagint and that passages like Hebrews 11:35 seem to echo the stories of martyrs in 2 Maccabees. They also point out that the New Testament never directly quotes several books everyone accepts, such as Esther and Song of Songs, so a lack of quotation does not settle the question. For Catholics, the church's long practice of reading these books in worship is itself strong evidence of their place in Scripture.

## The Orthodox view

The [[orthodox|Orthodox]] churches never had a single moment like Trent. Their Old Testament is the Septuagint as handed down in their worship. A synod held in Jerusalem in 1672 affirmed these books as part of Scripture. Orthodox Christians stress that the Bible lives within the church's worship and tradition, and that these books have been read and prayed in their [[liturgy]] for centuries. Some Orthodox writers distinguish the extra books slightly from the rest, while still receiving them as holy and worthy to be read.

## Holding it together

All three traditions are trying to be faithful to how God has given his word. They agree on the vast majority of the Old Testament and on the entire New Testament. If you are a new believer, it is perfectly fine to ask your church which view it holds and why.

In our key passage, Paul reminds the Roman Christians that the Scriptures were written to teach us, so that through endurance and the encouragement they give, we might have hope. He then prays that believers would live in harmony and glorify God with one voice. That is a good spirit in which to approach this question.
