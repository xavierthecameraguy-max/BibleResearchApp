Today there are dozens of English Bibles on the shelf. It is easy to forget that, for centuries, ordinary English speakers could not read the Bible in their own language at all. The story of how that changed is full of courage, scholarship and, sometimes, sacrifice.

## Before Wycliffe

For most of the Middle Ages, the Bible used in English churches was the Latin [[vulgate]]. Most people did not read Latin. There were parts of the Bible in Old English, such as translations of the Gospels and word-by-word glosses written between the lines of Latin Gospel books, but there was no full English Bible.

## Wycliffe: a Bible for ordinary people

In the 1380s, an Oxford scholar named John Wycliffe argued that every Christian should be able to read God's word. His followers produced the first complete Bibles in English: a first version around 1382 and a smoother revision some years later. Scholars debate how much of the translating Wycliffe did himself. The translators worked from the Latin Vulgate, because few in England then knew Hebrew or Greek.

These Bibles were copied by hand, since printing had not yet arrived. Church authorities feared that unauthorised translations would spread errors, and in 1409 they banned English translations without official approval. Even so, Wycliffe's Bibles were secretly copied and treasured.

## Tyndale: printed, and from the Greek

A century later, two things had changed. The printing press, from around 1450, made it possible to produce many copies quickly. And scholars were studying the Bible in its original languages again. In 1516 the scholar Erasmus published a printed Greek New Testament.

William Tyndale, an English scholar with a gift for language, resolved to translate the Bible directly from Hebrew and Greek. He reportedly told a critic that he would make a ploughboy know more Scripture than the clergyman did. Unable to work safely in England, he moved to Germany, and in 1526 his English New Testament was printed in Worms. Copies were smuggled into England in bales of cloth. Many were seized and burned.

Tyndale went on to translate much of the Old Testament from Hebrew. But in 1535 he was betrayed and arrested in Antwerp, then held in a castle near Brussels, and in 1536 he was executed. He is remembered as a [[martyr]] for the cause of an English Bible. His words, though, lived on. Phrases we still use, such as "let there be light" and "the powers that be", come down to us largely through Tyndale.

Within a few years, King Henry VIII allowed an official English Bible, the Great Bible of 1539, which drew heavily on Tyndale's work.

## Geneva and King James

During a time of persecution in England, Protestant exiles gathered in Geneva in Switzerland. There they produced the Geneva Bible in 1560. It was affordable, easy to read, divided into numbered verses, and full of helpful notes in the margins. It became the Bible of Shakespeare's England and travelled with the Pilgrims to America.

When King James I came to the throne, he did not like some of the Geneva notes. In 1604 he agreed to a new translation. About fifty scholars worked in teams and, building heavily on Tyndale and earlier versions, produced the Authorised Version of 1611, known today as the King James Version, or KJV.

The KJV became the best-loved English Bible for over three hundred years. Its rhythm and beauty shaped the English language itself. Most KJV Bibles printed today follow a standard edition from 1769, which updated spelling and corrected earlier printing errors.

## A Catholic English Bible

Catholic scholars, exiled from England, made their own English translation from the Latin Vulgate. The New Testament was published at Rheims in France in 1582, and the Old Testament at Douai in 1609–10. The Douay-Rheims Bible, later revised around 1750 by Bishop Richard Challoner, served English-speaking [[catholic|Catholics]] for generations.

## New discoveries, new translations

In the 1800s, scholars found and studied [[manuscript|manuscripts]] much older than those available to the KJV translators, such as Codex Sinaiticus. The English language had also changed. So a group of scholars produced the Revised Version in Britain (the full Bible appeared in 1885), with an American counterpart, the American Standard Version, in 1901.

The twentieth century brought a wave of new translations. The Revised Standard Version (complete in 1952) updated that tradition, and later led to the New Revised Standard Version (1989). Translators also began trying different approaches. The New International Version, published in full in 1978, aimed for accuracy in clear, natural English and became one of the most widely read Bibles in the world. The New King James Version (1982) updated the language of the KJV while keeping its familiar style.

More recently, the New Living Translation (1996) aimed for very readable everyday English. The English Standard Version (2001), a revision of the RSV, aimed to stay close to the original wording. The Christian Standard Bible (2017) sought a balance between the two. Catholic readers have the Jerusalem Bible and the New American Bible Revised Edition (2011), among others.

## Why so many?

It can seem confusing that there are so many English Bibles. But each one is an attempt to do what Wycliffe and Tyndale longed for: to let ordinary people hear God speak in their own language. In the next lesson, we will look at how these translations differ and how to choose one.

Our key passage, from Psalm 119, describes God's word as sweeter than honey and as a lamp to our feet. Many people risked a great deal so that you could hold that lamp in your own hands, in your own language.
