You have reached the last lesson in this track. We have travelled from ancient scrolls to modern bookshops. Now comes a very practical question: which Bible should you read?

If you have browsed a Bible app or bookshop, you may have seen a confusing jumble of letters: KJV, NIV, ESV, NLT, CSB and more. This lesson will help you make sense of them.

## Two ways to translate

Every [[translation]] involves choices. Languages do not line up neatly. Hebrew and Greek have different grammar, idioms and word order from English. Translators have to decide how closely to follow the original form and how much to reshape it into natural English.

Broadly, there are two approaches.

**Word-for-word**, also called [[formal-equivalence]]. The translator tries to follow the original words and structure as closely as English allows. The strength of this approach is that it lets you see more of the original wording, which is helpful for close study. The challenge is that the English can sometimes feel stiff or unusual.

**Thought-for-thought**, also called [[dynamic-equivalence]]. The translator aims to capture the meaning of each sentence and express it the way a modern English speaker naturally would, even if that means changing the wording or order. The strength is clarity and readability. The challenge is that the translator has to make more interpretive decisions for you.

Neither approach is "right" and the other "wrong". Both are trying to be faithful, just in different ways.

## An example

Here is a well-known phrase from 1 Peter 1:13. In Greek, Peter literally speaks of tying up "the loins of your mind". In the ancient world, people wore long robes, and before working or running they would gather the robe up and tuck it into a belt. Peter is using that picture for mental readiness.

The KJV keeps the picture almost exactly, telling readers to gird up the loins of their mind. Many modern translations instead say something like "prepare your minds for action" or "get your minds ready". The first keeps Peter's image so you can explore it. The second makes his meaning immediately clear. Both are doing honest work.

## A spectrum

Rather than two boxes, it is more helpful to think of a spectrum. Here is a rough guide, from more word-for-word to more thought-for-thought:

- **More word-for-word:** NASB (New American Standard Bible), ESV (English Standard Version), NKJV (New King James Version), KJV (King James Version).
- **In the middle:** CSB (Christian Standard Bible), NIV (New International Version). These aim for a balance between accuracy to the wording and natural readability.
- **More thought-for-thought:** NLT (New Living Translation), GNT (Good News Translation).

Then there are **paraphrases**, which go further still. A [[paraphrase|paraphrase]] retells the text in very free, contemporary language. The best known is *The Message* by Eugene Peterson, completed in 2002. Many people enjoy reading a paraphrase alongside a translation to see familiar passages in a fresh light.

These placements are approximate, and every translation makes a mix of choices. Catholic readers will also find excellent options such as the NABRE and editions of the NRSV, and the [[orthodox|Orthodox]] often use translations based on the Septuagint for the Old Testament.

## Tools inside your Bible

Most modern Bibles include **footnotes**. Look out for these, because they are a gift. A note beginning "Or" offers another possible way to translate a word. A note such as "Some manuscripts add" points to the textual variants we met in lesson 4. Notes like these show you the translators' honest work.

A **study Bible** goes further, adding introductions to each book, maps, timelines and explanations of difficult passages. Study Bibles can be very helpful for beginners, though remember that their notes are human comments, not Scripture itself.

## Why compare?

One of the best habits you can build is comparing translations. When a verse puzzles you, look at it in two or three versions. Where they agree, you can be confident about the meaning. Where they differ, you have found a spot where the original is rich or tricky, and that is worth exploring further. Bible apps make this easy.

## No perfect translation

It is worth saying plainly: there is no single perfect English translation. Every translation is made by gifted but imperfect people, and all of them face hard choices. The good news is that the major translations by recognised teams of scholars are trustworthy. God's word comes through clearly in all of them.

So how should you choose? Here are some gentle suggestions:

- Pick one main translation that you find readable and will actually read regularly.
- When studying closely, compare it with one from a different part of the spectrum.
- Ask your church which translation it uses in worship. Reading the same one can help you follow along.
- Talk it over with a pastor or a more experienced believer.

## Someone to guide you

Our key passage tells of an Ethiopian official reading the prophet Isaiah on a desert road. He had the Scriptures in his hands, yet when Philip asked if he understood, he answered honestly: how could he, unless someone guided him? Philip then explained the good news about Jesus from that very passage.

That is a beautiful picture for any new believer. The best translation is the one you read, and you do not have to read it alone. God has given you his word, his Spirit and his people to help you understand it. Open it with confidence and enjoy the journey.
