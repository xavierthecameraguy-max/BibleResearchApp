When you read your Bible in English, you are reading a [[translation]]. The books were first written long ago in languages very different from ours. Knowing a little about those languages, and the people who wrote in them, helps the Bible come alive.

## Three languages

**Hebrew.** Most of the Old Testament was written in [[hebrew]], the language of ancient Israel. Hebrew is written from right to left, and in its earliest form it was written with consonants only, with readers supplying the vowel sounds from memory. It is a vivid, concrete language, full of pictures. "Anger", for example, is often described with a phrase about a hot or flaring nose.

**Aramaic.** A few parts of the Old Testament are written in [[aramaic]], a close cousin of Hebrew. The largest sections are in the books of Daniel and Ezra, written during or after the time when many Jews lived under foreign empires where Aramaic was the common tongue. By Jesus' day, Aramaic was probably the language he spoke at home. The Gospels even keep a few of his Aramaic words, such as *talitha koum*, "little girl, get up" (Mark 5:41), and *Abba*, a warm word for "Father".

**Greek.** The New Testament was written in [[koine-greek]]. *Koine* means "common". This was not the polished Greek of famous philosophers and poets but the ordinary Greek used in markets and letters across the eastern Roman Empire. That is worth pausing over: God's message about Jesus was written in the everyday language of ordinary people.

## Written by God, written by people

Christians believe the Bible is God's word. But it did not drop from the sky, and the writers were not robots. The word Christians use is [[inspiration]].

In 2 Timothy 3:16, Paul says all Scripture is "God-breathed". In our key passage, Peter says the prophets spoke from God as they were "carried along" by the Holy Spirit, a picture like a ship moved by the wind. The writers still used their own minds, vocabulary and style.

You can see this human side throughout the Bible. Luke tells us he carefully investigated everything and drew on accounts handed down by eyewitnesses (Luke 1:1-4). Paul sometimes used a secretary. One of them, Tertius, even sends his own greeting in Romans 16:22. The psalm writers pour out real joy and real grief. Christians believe God was at work in all of it, so that the result is both fully human writing and truly God's message.

## Who wrote the books?

For many books, tradition passed down by Jewish and Christian communities names an author. Moses is linked with the first five books, David with many psalms, Solomon with much of Proverbs, and the apostles and their close companions with the New Testament.

Some books name their author plainly. Most of Paul's letters begin with his name. Others, including the four Gospels and the letter to the Hebrews, do not actually name their writer in the text. The names we use come from early church tradition, which in the case of the Gospels goes back to at least the second century.

Here is where it helps to be honest: for some books, scholars hold different views. A few examples:

- **The Pentateuch** (Genesis to Deuteronomy). Tradition links these books with Moses. Many scholars think they were gathered and edited over several centuries, drawing on older sources. Others see Moses as the main source, with later editors adding touches such as the account of his death in Deuteronomy 34. Many hold views somewhere in between.
- **Isaiah.** The book is named after a prophet who lived in Jerusalem in the 700s BC. Many scholars think chapters 40 to 66, which speak to people in exile in Babylon some 150 years later, came from later prophets in Isaiah's tradition. Others hold that Isaiah himself wrote the whole book, looking ahead by God's revelation.
- **Some New Testament letters.** Almost all scholars agree Paul wrote letters such as Romans, Galatians and 1 Corinthians. For a few others, including Ephesians, Colossians and the letters to Timothy and Titus, opinion is divided. Some think a follower may have written in Paul's name, a practice known in the ancient world. 2 Peter is also debated.

These conversations can sound unsettling at first, so it may help to know a few things. Scholars who disagree on these questions are often careful, sincere people, and many committed Christians are among them on every side. Many believers hold firmly to the traditional authors and have thoughtful reasons for doing so. Others are more open. Across all these views, Christians have recognised these books as Scripture for many centuries. The church's confidence rests on God's work through the writings, not only on knowing every human detail.

## Why this matters for you

Knowing that the Bible came through real people, real languages and real places helps you read it well. When Paul writes to the church in Corinth, he is writing to actual people with actual problems. When a psalm cries out "How long, O Lord?", a real person felt that. This is why paying attention to [[context]] matters so much.

Peter's words in our key passage are a good place to rest. He reminds his readers that he was not passing on clever stories but things he saw with his own eyes. And he says the prophetic message is like a lamp shining in a dark place. However it came to us, through Hebrew scrolls, Aramaic chapters and Greek letters, Christians believe that lamp is God's gift, given so that we can see our way.
