In the last lesson we saw that, for centuries, the Bible was copied by hand. That naturally raises a question: how do we know the Bible we read today matches what was first written? The answer is one of the most fascinating stories in the history of the Bible, and it involves a goat, a cave and a shepherd.

## A discovery in the desert

In 1947, according to the usual account, a young Bedouin shepherd was searching for a lost animal in the dry, rocky hills near the Dead Sea. He threw a stone into a cave and heard something shatter. Inside were clay jars, and inside the jars were ancient scrolls wrapped in linen.

Over the next decade, searchers explored eleven caves near a site called Qumran. They found the remains of around 900 scrolls, most of them in fragments, some as small as a fingernail. These are known as the [[dead-sea-scrolls]]. They date from roughly 250 BC to AD 70, and many scholars connect them with a Jewish community that lived nearby, though exactly who they were is still discussed.

About a quarter of the scrolls are copies of books of the Hebrew Bible. Every book of the Hebrew Bible is represented, at least in fragments, except Esther. (No piece of Nehemiah has been identified either, but in ancient times Ezra and Nehemiah were counted as one book, and Ezra is there.) Before 1947, the oldest substantial Hebrew copies of the Old Testament dated to around AD 900 to 1000. Suddenly, scholars had copies that were roughly a thousand years older.

## The Great Isaiah Scroll

The star of the collection is the Great Isaiah Scroll. It is over seven metres long, contains all 66 chapters of Isaiah, and is usually dated to around the second century BC. It is one of the oldest complete books of the Bible in existence.

What did it show? When scholars compared it with the medieval Hebrew text, the [[masoretic-text]], they found the two agree to a remarkable degree. The differences are mostly spellings, small grammatical changes and the occasional word. Across a thousand years of hand copying, the message of Isaiah had been preserved with great faithfulness.

It is fair to add that the scrolls also show some variety. For a few books, such as Jeremiah, Qumran held more than one form of the text, including a shorter one closer to the ancient Greek translation. This has helped scholars understand the Bible's early history more richly. But the overall picture is one of a text handled with great care.

Our key passage, from Isaiah 40, comes from this very book. It contrasts grass that withers and flowers that fade with the word of God, which stands forever. Holding a two-thousand-year-old copy of those very lines is a moving illustration of the point.

## A wealth of New Testament manuscripts

The New Testament has its own remarkable story. Scholars know of more than 5,000 Greek [[manuscript|manuscripts]] of all or part of the New Testament, plus many thousands of copies in early translations such as Latin, Syriac and Coptic. For comparison, many famous works of ancient Greek and Roman literature survive in only a handful of copies, often made many centuries after the author lived.

Some New Testament manuscripts are very early. A small scrap of papyrus known as P52, kept in Manchester, England, contains a few lines from John 18. It is often dated to somewhere around AD 125 to 175, although scholars debate this and some argue for a later date. Either way, it is among the earliest pieces of the New Testament we have. From the fourth century come great complete or nearly complete Bibles, such as Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus.

## Comparing the copies

With so many manuscripts, it is no surprise that they contain differences. These are called [[textual-variant|textual variants]]. The careful work of comparing manuscripts to work out the original wording is called [[textual-criticism]].

Scholars weigh questions such as: Which reading appears in the oldest copies? Which is found across many different regions? Which reading best explains how the others came about? A tired copyist is more likely to shorten a sentence by accident than to invent a new one, for example.

The great majority of variants are minor: spelling differences, changes in word order that do not affect meaning in Greek, or a word like "the" being present or absent. Only a small portion make any difference to meaning, and most scholars, from many backgrounds, agree that no central Christian teaching depends on a single uncertain passage.

## Notes in your Bible

You may notice a few places where your Bible adds brackets or a note such as "some early manuscripts do not include these verses". Two well-known examples are:

- **Mark 16:9-20.** Some of the oldest manuscripts end Mark's Gospel at 16:8, with the women leaving the empty tomb in awe. The longer ending appears in most later copies. Most scholars think it was added after Mark wrote, though some argue it is original.
- **John 7:53–8:11.** The story of Jesus and the woman caught in adultery is missing from the earliest manuscripts and appears in different places in some later ones. Many scholars think it is a genuine early memory about Jesus that was added to John's Gospel later.

Modern Bibles usually still print these passages but mark them honestly. That openness is a strength. It means translators are showing you their work rather than hiding it.

The overall story is encouraging. Through scrolls hidden in caves and thousands of patient copies, the words of Scripture have been handed down to us with great care. As Isaiah says, the grass withers, but the word of our God endures.
