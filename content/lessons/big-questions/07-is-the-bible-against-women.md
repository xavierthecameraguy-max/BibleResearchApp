Most Bible readers eventually come across passages about women that are hard to read. Some tell stories of terrible violence. Others seem to say that women should be silent in church or submit to their husbands. You may also have heard that the Bible, or the church, is simply against women.

It is an honest question that deserves an honest answer. This lesson will not make every difficulty disappear, but it will help you read these passages in their [[context]].

## Where the Bible starts

The Bible's first word about humanity is that God created people male and female, both bearing the [[image-of-god|image of God]] and both given the task of caring for the world (Genesis 1:27-28). In Genesis 2 the woman is called a helper for the man. The Hebrew word, *ezer*, does not mean a servant. It is often used of God himself, who helps his people (Psalm 121:1-2).

Conflict between men and women appears as a result of the fall (Genesis 3:16). Many Christians read this as a description of what went wrong, not as God's ideal.

## Stories that describe, not approve

The Old Testament tells some horrifying stories, such as the abuse and murder of an unnamed woman in Judges 19 and the rape of Tamar in 2 Samuel 13. The scholar Phyllis Trible called such passages "texts of terror" and urged readers not to look away. Telling a story is not the same as approving it. Judges keeps repeating that everyone did what was right in their own eyes (Judges 21:25), a sign that things had gone badly wrong.

There are also remarkable women in the Old Testament: Miriam the prophet (Exodus 15:20); Deborah, who led Israel as a judge (Judges 4-5); Huldah, the prophet whom the king's officials consulted (2 Kings 22:14-20); and Ruth and Esther, who each have a book named after them. Some Old Testament laws reflect a world where men held most of the power. Christians often read them in light of [[progressive-revelation|progressive revelation]], with Jesus bringing the fullest light.

## Jesus and women

Jesus treated women in ways that surprised people. He taught Mary of Bethany as she sat at his feet, the place of a disciple (Luke 10:38-42). He talked at length with a Samaritan woman, to his disciples' astonishment (John 4:27). Women travelled with him and supported his work from their own money (Luke 8:1-3). Women stayed near the cross when most of the men had fled, and were the first witnesses of the [[resurrection]]. Jesus sent Mary Magdalene to tell the others he was alive (John 20:17-18). Orthodox Christians honour her as "equal to the apostles", and Catholics call her the "apostle to the apostles".

## Women in the early church

At [[pentecost|Pentecost]], Peter announced that sons and daughters would prophesy (Acts 2:17-18). Priscilla and her husband Aquila explained the faith more fully to a gifted preacher named Apollos (Acts 18:26). Lydia hosted a church in her home (Acts 16:14-15, 40).

Romans 16, this lesson's key passage, is full of women. Paul commends Phoebe, a deacon or servant (translations differ) of the church at Cenchreae, who probably carried his letter to Rome. He greets Prisca, Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa and Persis, who worked hard in the Lord. He also greets Junia, whom he calls prominent among the apostles (Romans 16:7). Most scholars now agree that Junia was a woman, though they debate whether the phrase means she was an apostle or was well known to the apostles.

## The hard passages in context

**1 Corinthians 14:34-35** says women should be silent in the churches. Yet earlier in the same letter Paul assumes women will pray and prophesy aloud in worship (1 Corinthians 11:5). So most scholars think he is limiting a particular kind of speaking rather than banning women from speaking at all. Some suggest disruptive questions; many [[complementarian|complementarians]] suggest the public weighing of prophecies (1 Corinthians 14:29). Others think these verses were added by a later copyist, since some manuscripts place them after verse 40, though no surviving manuscript leaves them out.

**1 Timothy 2:11-15** says that a woman should not teach or have authority over a man. Some read this as a lasting principle rooted in creation, since the passage refers to Adam and Eve. Others note that the letter is responding to false teachers in Ephesus (1 Timothy 1:3-7) and see a local instruction. Notice the opening command too: women should learn, which was not taken for granted then.

**Ephesians 5:21-33** tells wives to submit to their husbands. But it opens by calling all believers to submit to one another (Ephesians 5:21), and it asks husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her. In a world where husbands held enormous power, that was a radical call to self-giving love. Complementarians see here a pattern of loving, servant leadership by husbands; [[egalitarian|egalitarians]] see mutual submission as the key to the whole passage.

## Two views today

Faithful Christians who love the Bible reach different conclusions about women's roles:

- **[[complementarian|Complementarians]]** believe men and women are equal in worth, but that God gives some different roles. Usually this means that only men serve as pastors or elders, and that husbands are called to loving, servant leadership at home. They see this as rooted in creation, not just culture. Many Baptist, Reformed and other evangelical churches hold this view.
- **[[egalitarian|Egalitarians]]** believe men and women are equal in worth and that all roles in church and home are open to both, according to their gifts. They point to Genesis 1, Galatians 3:28, Deborah, Priscilla, Phoebe and Junia, and read the restrictive passages as addressing local situations. Many Methodist, Anglican, Lutheran and Pentecostal churches ordain women.

The **Catholic and Orthodox** churches ordain only men as priests and bishops, pointing to Jesus' choice of twelve men and to the church's long tradition. They also honour women deeply, above all Mary, and many women saints, martyrs and teachers.

On every side, Christians agree that women and men are equally loved by God, and that no Bible passage excuses abuse. If you are being harmed at home or at church, please tell someone you trust and seek safety and help.

These questions are worth talking through with a pastor or mentor, and with believers who see them differently.
