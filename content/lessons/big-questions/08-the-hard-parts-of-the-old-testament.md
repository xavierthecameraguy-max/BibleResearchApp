If you have started reading the Old Testament, you may already have stumbled. One chapter tells a beautiful story of rescue; the next describes a city destroyed, a law about slaves, or a ban on clothes woven from two kinds of fabric (Leviticus 19:19). Some passages command Israel to wipe out whole peoples (Deuteronomy 7:1-2; 1 Samuel 15:3). It is natural to ask: is this really the same God we meet in Jesus?

If these passages trouble you, you are not alone, and your unease is not a sign of weak faith. Christians have wrestled with them since the earliest centuries. This lesson offers some ways of reading them that many believers have found helpful.

## An old question

Around AD 140, a teacher named Marcion decided that the God of the Old Testament was a harsh, lesser god, not the loving Father of Jesus. He discarded the Old Testament entirely, along with much of what became the New. The wider church rejected his teaching as a [[heresy]], insisting that the God of Israel, who describes himself as compassionate and gracious, slow to anger (Exodus 34:6), is the same God who comes to us in Jesus.

So the church kept the hard parts, and kept wrestling with them.

## Remember the context

The Old Testament was written over many centuries, and reading it well means noticing its [[context]].

Israel's laws were given in the ancient Near East, where slavery, warfare and harsh punishment were normal. Compared with other ancient law codes, such as Hammurabi's laws from Babylon (about 1750 BC), Israel's laws often stand out for protecting the vulnerable:

- Foreigners, widows and orphans were not to be mistreated (Exodus 22:21-22).
- An Israelite in debt-slavery was to be freed after six years, with generous provisions (Deuteronomy 15:12-14).
- An escaped slave was not to be handed back to his master (Deuteronomy 23:15-16).

That does not make every law comfortable. Slavery was regulated, not abolished, foreign slaves were treated less generously than Israelites (Leviticus 25:44-46), and some penalties seem very severe. But Jesus gives an important clue. When asked about divorce, he said Moses allowed it because of the people's hardness of heart, but it was not that way from the beginning (Matthew 19:8). Some laws, in other words, were not God's ideal but limits placed on a broken world, a step along the way.

What about the strange laws on food, fabrics and skin diseases? Many were about marking Israel out as a holy people (Leviticus 11:44-45). The New Testament teaches that these laws are fulfilled in Jesus. Mark notes that Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19), and the early church decided that Gentile believers need not keep the whole law of Moses (Acts 15). The "Reading Law" lesson in How to Read the Bible explores this further.

## The conquest stories

The hardest passages describe the conquest of Canaan, where Israel is told to "devote to destruction" whole cities, sometimes including women and children (Deuteronomy 20:16-17; Joshua 6:21). Christians respond in several ways.

**Judgement after long patience.** The Bible presents these events as God's judgement on cultures that practised things such as child sacrifice (Deuteronomy 12:31), after centuries of waiting (Genesis 15:16). It was not because Israel was better (Deuteronomy 9:4-5). Israel was warned it would face the same judgement if it did the same (Leviticus 18:24-28), and later it did, in the [[exile]].

**Exaggerated war language.** Ancient war reports often used sweeping language even when many people survived. Joshua says whole regions were wiped out (Joshua 10:40), yet Judges describes many Canaanites still living there (Judges 1:21-36). Scholars such as Paul Copan argue that this was a familiar way of describing a decisive victory. (Historians also debate how Israel actually settled the land.)

**Israel's growing understanding.** Some Christian scholars, such as Peter Enns, suggest that Israel told its story the way ancient peoples did, picturing God as a warrior fighting for his nation. On this view the Bible honestly records Israel's growing understanding of God, which reaches its fullness in Jesus. Other Christians think this goes too far, holding that God really did command these events, but for a unique moment never to be repeated.

**Reading spiritually.** Early Christian writers such as Origen, in the third century, also read these battles as pictures of the believer's fight against sin. Many Christians, especially Orthodox and Catholic, still value this way of reading, often alongside the others.

Sadly, these texts have sometimes been misused to justify violence. Christians of every tradition today agree that they give no licence for that. And even here there is mercy. Rahab, a Canaanite, is welcomed into Israel (Joshua 6:25), and later God spares Nineveh, the capital of Israel's enemy, because he cares for its people (Jonah 4:11).

## Progressive revelation

Many Christians find the idea of [[progressive-revelation|progressive revelation]] helpful. God made himself known step by step, as a good teacher leads students from simple lessons to deeper ones. Hebrews 1:1-3 puts it like this: long ago God spoke in many ways through the prophets, but now he has spoken through his Son, who shows us exactly what God is like.

This does not mean the Old Testament God is a different or worse God. The Old Testament is full of mercy, and the New Testament speaks of judgement too. But it does mean Christians read the whole Bible in the light of Jesus. He came not to abolish the Law but to fulfil it (Matthew 5:17), and he called his followers to love even their enemies (Matthew 5:43-44). He shows where the whole story was heading.

## Wrestling honestly

In Genesis 32, Jacob wrestles all night with a mysterious figure and refuses to let go until he is blessed, a good picture of handling hard passages. You do not have to pretend they are easy, or give up. You can:

- Read the passage in its context.
- Bring your questions to God in honest prayer.
- Use a good study Bible, and talk it over with a pastor, priest or mentor.
- Be willing to say, "I don't understand this yet."

Faithful Christians do not all agree about these passages, but they share a conviction that God is good, that Jesus shows us his heart, and that it is better to keep wrestling with the Bible than to put it down.
