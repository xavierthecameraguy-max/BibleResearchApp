If you are going to build your life on Jesus, it is fair to ask how we know what he said and did. Almost everything we know about him comes from the four [[gospel|Gospels]]: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. Can they be trusted? This lesson looks at the main evidence, and at where scholars still disagree.

## What kind of books are they?

Luke begins his Gospel by explaining his method (Luke 1:1-4). Many people had already written accounts, he says, based on what had been handed down by those who were eyewitnesses from the start. He has investigated everything carefully, so that his reader can be confident about what he has been taught.

That tells us something important. The Gospel writers did not think they were writing fairy tales. Many scholars today, including some who are not Christians, compare the Gospels with ancient biographies, a kind of writing that aimed to show the character of an important person through his words and deeds. Ancient biographies were not like modern ones. They often arranged events by theme rather than in strict order, and they summarised speeches rather than giving word-for-word transcripts. Knowing this helps explain why the Gospels sometimes tell the same story in slightly different ways.

## When were they written?

Jesus was crucified around AD 30 or 33. Most scholars date the Gospels within the following decades:

- **Mark:** around AD 65-70, usually thought to be the earliest.
- **Matthew and Luke:** around AD 75-90.
- **John:** around AD 90-95, though some suggest a little later.

These are estimates, and there is a real range of opinion. Some scholars argue for earlier dates, partly because the book of Acts, Luke's second volume, ends with Paul still alive in Rome around AD 62. Others place some Gospels a little later.

Either way, the gap is short by ancient standards. The main surviving accounts of the life of Alexander the Great were written roughly three to four and a half centuries after his death, yet historians still use them. The Gospels were written within the lifetime of people who had known Jesus.

Paul's letters are even earlier, from the AD 50s. In 1 Corinthians 15:3-7, written around AD 53-55, Paul passes on a summary of the faith that he says he himself had received: Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day and appeared to many. Many scholars think this summary goes back to within a few years of the crucifixion.

## Eyewitnesses and memory

Between Jesus' ministry and the written Gospels, the stories were passed on by word of mouth. Some people imagine this was like a game of "telephone", where a message gets garbled. But first-century Jewish culture valued careful memorisation, and Jesus' teaching, full of vivid [[parable|parables]] and short sayings, was easy to remember. Many people who had seen and heard Jesus were still alive and leading the churches.

An early church leader named Papias, writing around AD 100-130, reported that Mark wrote down what Peter had preached. The British scholar Richard Bauckham has argued in detail that the Gospels preserve eyewitness testimony, pointing to clues such as the names of minor characters who may have been known to the first readers.

Others are more cautious. The American scholar Bart Ehrman, who is not a Christian, points out that memories can shift over time and that the Gospel writers shaped their material. These are serious conversations, and you will meet both views if you read further.

## How well were they copied?

We do not have the original documents, which is true of every ancient book. We have copies. For the New Testament there are more than 5,000 handwritten Greek [[manuscript|manuscripts]], plus thousands more in early translations such as Latin, Syriac and Coptic. That is far more than for any other ancient writing. Some fragments are very early: a small scrap of John known as P52 is often dated somewhere around AD 125-175.

The copies do contain differences, called [[textual-variant|textual variants]]. The vast majority are minor: spelling, word order or an accidentally skipped word. A few are more significant, such as the longer ending of Mark (Mark 16:9-20) and the story of the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53-8:11), and most modern Bibles mark these with a note. By carefully comparing manuscripts, a discipline called [[textual-criticism|textual criticism]], scholars of many backgrounds agree that we can know what the authors wrote in the great majority of places. No central Christian belief depends on a disputed passage.

## What archaeology adds

Archaeology cannot prove that Jesus worked miracles or rose from the dead. But it can show whether the Gospels fit the real world of first-century Judea and Galilee, and on many points they do:

- A stone found at Caesarea in 1961 bears the name of Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor who condemned Jesus.
- The Pool of Bethesda, which John describes as having five covered colonnades (John 5:2), has been excavated in Jerusalem.
- The Pool of Siloam (John 9:7) was uncovered in 2004.
- A bone box found in 1990, inscribed with the name Caiaphas, is thought by many scholars to have belonged to the high priest of that name, though not all agree.

Writers outside the church mention Jesus too. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing around AD 116, says that Christ was executed under Pilate. The Jewish historian Josephus also mentions Jesus, though most scholars think later Christian copyists added to one of his passages.

## Honest questions that remain

Scholars still debate who exactly wrote each Gospel. The Gospels do not name their authors in the text itself, and the traditional names were attached early, probably in the second century. Many scholars accept the traditional authors, while others think the books came from followers of those figures or from other early Christians. Readers also notice differences between the Gospels, such as details of who visited the tomb on Easter morning. Christians usually explain these as different witnesses telling the story from different angles, as ancient biographers did. Sceptical scholars sometimes see them as contradictions.

You do not need to settle every debate to see that the Gospels are serious, early documents rooted in history. Luke wrote so that his reader could have confidence. Many careful people, after weighing the evidence, have found the same.
