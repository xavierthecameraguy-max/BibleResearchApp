Christianity stands or falls on one claim: that Jesus of Nazareth, crucified under Pontius Pilate, rose bodily from the dead. Paul was blunt about it. If Christ has not been raised, he wrote, our faith is futile (1 Corinthians 15:14, 17). This lesson explains why Christians believe the [[resurrection]] really happened, describes the main alternative explanations, and asks how evidence and faith fit together.

## What almost all historians agree on

Historians of many backgrounds, whether Christian, Jewish, agnostic or atheist, agree on a few basic facts:

- Jesus was a real Jewish teacher who was crucified by the Romans in Jerusalem around AD 30-33.
- Soon afterwards, his followers became convinced that he had risen and appeared to them.
- This belief spread quickly, even in Jerusalem, where he had been executed.
- Two people who had not been his followers, Jesus' brother James and a persecutor named Saul (later Paul), came to believe as well.

The debate is usually not about these facts. It is about how best to explain them.

## The case Christians make

**A very early report.** In 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, written around AD 53-55, Paul hands on a summary he had received. It lists appearances to Peter, to the Twelve, to more than five hundred people at once, to James, to all the apostles and finally to Paul himself. Many scholars think Paul learned this summary within a few years of the crucifixion, perhaps when he visited Peter and James in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18-19). Paul even notes that most of the five hundred were still alive, as if to say: go and ask them.

**The empty tomb.** All four Gospels say that women found Jesus' tomb empty on the Sunday morning. Christians point out that in that culture women's testimony was often given less weight. Someone inventing the story would hardly choose women as the key witnesses. The earliest opponents also did not claim the body was still in the tomb. According to Matthew 28:11-15, they said the disciples had stolen it, which assumes the tomb was empty. Some scholars doubt the empty tomb accounts and think they developed later, but many, including some who are not Christians, consider them historically likely.

**The appearances.** The accounts describe Jesus appearing to individuals and to groups, indoors and outdoors, to friends and to doubters, over several weeks. They describe him as physically real, yet changed. He ate fish (Luke 24:42-43), yet could appear in a locked room (John 20:19).

**The transformation.** On the night Jesus was arrested, his followers ran away, and Peter denied knowing him. Within weeks they were preaching boldly in public, and many later suffered for it. Believers began meeting on Sunday, the day of the resurrection, and spoke of Jesus as Lord. People may die for something false that they sincerely believe, but it is hard to see why so many would suffer for something they knew they had made up.

**A surprising belief.** Many Jews in Jesus' day did expect a resurrection, but they expected all God's people to rise together at the end of history. No one expected one person to rise in the middle of history, ahead of everyone else. The scholar N. T. Wright has argued that this unexpected belief is best explained if something really happened.

## The main alternative explanations

It is only fair to describe the other explanations people have offered, and why Christians are not persuaded by them.

- **The body was stolen, or the disciples lied.** This was the earliest alternative. Christians respond that the disciples' willingness to suffer makes deliberate fraud unlikely.
- **The women went to the wrong tomb.** But then the authorities could simply have pointed to the right one.
- **Jesus did not really die.** This "swoon" idea suggests he revived in the cool of the tomb. Most historians reject it. Roman soldiers were practised at execution, and a badly injured man would hardly have inspired faith in a glorious victory over death.
- **The appearances were visions or grief experiences.** This is the most common view among sceptical scholars today. Grieving people do sometimes sense the presence of someone they have lost. Some scholars, such as the German writer Gerd Lüdemann, have suggested that Peter and Paul had visions that others then shared. Christians respond that this struggles to explain group appearances, the conversion of a hostile Paul and the empty tomb.
- **The story grew into a legend over time.** Christians point to how early Paul's summary is.

Some historians add that, whatever happened, historical study by its nature cannot conclude that a miracle took place. At that point the question becomes a larger one: does God exist, and might he act in the world?

It is also worth saying, respectfully, that other faiths see these events differently. Jewish tradition does not accept that Jesus was raised as the [[messiah|Messiah]]. Most Muslims believe Jesus was not crucified at all, but was taken up to God. These are sincere convictions held by many thoughtful people.

## Faith and evidence

Evidence can show that faith in the resurrection is reasonable. It cannot force anyone to believe, and people who look at the same facts reach different conclusions. In the end, believing that Jesus is risen is not only about weighing arguments. It is about trusting a person.

That is where Thomas, from the first lesson in this track, comes back in. He asked for evidence, and Jesus met him. But Thomas's response was not "the theory holds up". It was worship. Christians believe the risen Jesus still meets people today, through the testimony of Scripture, through the [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]] and through his people.

Paul draws out what this means. Because Christ has been raised, he is the first of a great harvest (1 Corinthians 15:20). Death is not the end, and all who belong to him will one day be raised too. Everything else in the Christian faith grows out of that first Easter morning.
