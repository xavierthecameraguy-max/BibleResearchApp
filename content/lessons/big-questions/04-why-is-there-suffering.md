Sooner or later, every believer meets this question, often not in a classroom but in a hospital corridor, at a graveside or while watching the news. If God is good and all-powerful, why is there so much suffering? For many people, of every faith and none, it is the hardest question of all. Some sincere and thoughtful people have stepped away from faith because of it.

If you are suffering right now, please hear this first: this lesson is not meant to explain your pain away. Christians do not have a neat formula. What we do have is a God who does not stay at a distance, and a long tradition of honest wrestling.

## Naming the problem

The question is very old. Ancient Greek thinkers asked it, and so does the Bible. Job argues with God through chapter after chapter. Habakkuk asks why God seems to tolerate injustice (Habakkuk 1:2-4). In 1710 the philosopher Gottfried Leibniz coined the word [[theodicy]] for an attempt to explain how a good God can allow evil.

It helps to notice two kinds of suffering. Some comes from what people do to one another: cruelty, war, abuse and neglect. Some comes from the natural world: earthquakes, cancer and floods. Christians have offered several thoughts about both. Most would say these thoughts are like lamps in a dark room. They help, but they do not light every corner.

## Some things Christians have said

**Love needs freedom.** Many Christians, from Augustine around AD 400 to C. S. Lewis in the twentieth century, have pointed out that God made people able to love. Real love cannot be forced, so God gave us real freedom, and with it the terrible possibility of choosing to harm. A world of puppets would have no cruelty, but it would have no love either. Not every Christian puts the weight here. Many in the Reformed tradition stress instead that God is sovereign even over evil, without being its author, and weaves it into his good purposes, as Joseph told the brothers who had sold him: you meant it for harm, but God meant it for good (Genesis 50:20).

**A world out of joint.** Genesis 3 describes humans turning away from God, and [[sin]] damaging everything: our relationship with God, with one another and with creation itself. Paul writes that the whole creation is groaning, as if in childbirth, waiting to be set free (Romans 8:19-22). Christians differ on how natural disasters fit in. Some believe death and disaster entered the world through human sin. Others think a world of moving continents and changing weather was part of a good but unfinished creation, still on its way to what God intends. Either way, the Bible insists that things are not as they should be.

That does not mean a person's suffering is a punishment for their own sins. Jesus firmly rejected that idea (John 9:1-3; Luke 13:1-5). Job's friends insisted he must have deserved his troubles, and God said they were wrong (Job 42:7).

**Growth through hardship.** Irenaeus, a second-century bishop honoured by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians alike, saw the world as a place where people grow towards maturity. The New Testament says suffering can produce endurance, character and hope (Romans 5:3-5). Many believers look back and see this in their own lives. But it is something to discover for yourself, not something to tell a grieving friend.

**We cannot see everything.** When God finally speaks to Job, he does not give an explanation. He shows Job the wonders of creation and invites him to trust the One who made them (Job 38-42). Job's questions are not all answered, but he comes away having met God (Job 42:5).

## Permission to lament

The Bible gives us more than ideas. It gives us prayers. About a third of the Psalms are [[lament|laments]], and a whole book, Lamentations, mourns the destruction of Jerusalem. These prayers protest, question and weep, and they are part of Scripture.

At the tomb of his friend Lazarus, Jesus wept (John 11:35), even though he was about to raise him. He did not hurry past grief, and neither do we have to. Paul says that when we do not know how to pray, the [[holy-spirit|Holy Spirit]] prays for us with groaning that words cannot express (Romans 8:26).

## God in the suffering

For Christians, the deepest response to suffering is not an argument but a person. Christians believe that in Jesus, God entered our pain himself. He was betrayed, mocked, beaten and executed. On the cross he prayed the opening words of a lament psalm: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34).

The cross does not explain why any particular thing happens. But it shows that God is not indifferent. Paul reasons that if God did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us, he will not abandon us now (Romans 8:31-32). Many Christians, especially in Catholic and Orthodox traditions, also speak of uniting their sufferings with Christ's, so that even pain can become a kind of prayer (Colossians 1:24).

The story does not end at the cross. The [[resurrection]] is God's promise that suffering will not have the last word. The Bible's final picture is of God wiping every tear from his people's eyes, with no more death, mourning or pain (Revelation 21:4). Romans 8:28 is often quoted, sometimes too quickly. It does not say that everything that happens is good. It says that God is at work in all things for the good of those who love him.

## Walking with people who suffer

Job's friends did their best work in the first week, when they sat with him in silence (Job 2:13). Things went wrong when they started explaining. Well-meant phrases such as "everything happens for a reason" can wound someone in pain. Often the most Christian thing to do is to show up, listen, pray and help in practical ways. Paul simply says to weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15).

If you are carrying a heavy grief or a hard question, do not carry it alone. Talk with a pastor, priest or mentor, and if the weight feels overwhelming, a doctor or counsellor can help too. Asking for help is not a failure of faith.
