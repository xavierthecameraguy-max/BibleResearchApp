If you open your Bible somewhere in the middle, chances are you will land in poetry. The Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Lamentations and large parts of the prophets are all written as poems. Roughly a third of the Old Testament is poetry.

Poetry works differently from a news report or a letter. It is meant to be felt as well as understood. Learning how it works will open up some of the most loved parts of Scripture.

## How Hebrew poetry works

English poems often rhyme sounds. Hebrew poems rhyme *ideas*. This is called **[[parallelism|parallelism]]**: two (or sometimes three) lines are paired, and the second responds to the first. It can do this in several ways.

- **Echo:** the second line says the same thing in different words. "The heavens declare God's glory; the sky shows what his hands have made" (see Psalm 19:1).
- **Contrast:** the second line gives the opposite. Proverbs does this often: the wise do one thing, but fools do another.
- **Build:** the second line takes the thought further or adds a picture.

Once you spot parallelism, you will stop treating each line as a separate statement and see the pairs as one thought. It also helps with puzzling words: if one line is unclear, its partner often explains it.

## Imagery and emotion

Poetry loves pictures. God is a rock, a shepherd, a fortress, a mother bird sheltering her chicks. Enemies are lions and floodwaters. These images are not meant to be read like a science textbook. They tell us something true by appealing to imagination.

Poetry is also full of emotion: joy, anger, fear, longing, exhaustion. The Bible does not hide strong feelings. It gives them words and turns them toward God.

## The Psalms

The book of Psalms was Israel's songbook and prayer book, collected over many centuries. Christians of every tradition still pray it. Many monasteries and churches pray through all 150 psalms regularly in their [[liturgy]], as Jewish communities have long done in their own worship.

Psalms come in different types. Three of the main ones are:

**[[lament|Lament]].** These are the most common. The writer cries out in pain, confusion or complaint, often asking "How long?" Most laments move, sometimes suddenly, toward trust and praise.

**Praise.** These celebrate who God is: creator, king, faithful and good (for example, Psalm 145 and Psalm 150).

**Thanksgiving.** These thank God for a specific rescue or gift (for example, Psalm 30).

Look at Psalm 13, the key passage. It begins with four anguished questions: How long will God forget me? How long will he hide his face? Then the writer asks God to answer. And it ends with a decision to trust in God's steadfast love and to sing. In six short verses, it walks from despair to hope, without pretending the pain was not real.

That is a gift for new believers. You are allowed to be honest with God. The Psalms give you words when you have none.

## Proverbs: wisdom, not guarantees

The book of Proverbs belongs to what is called [[wisdom-literature|wisdom literature]]: writing that teaches how to live skilfully in God's world. Its starting point is "the fear of the Lord," meaning deep reverence and trust (Proverbs 1:7).

Proverbs are short, memorable sayings about how life generally works. Hard work tends to bring reward. Gentle words tend to calm anger. Raising a child well usually shapes their path (Proverbs 22:6).

But proverbs are not promises that always come true in every case. Sometimes two proverbs even sit side by side and seem to pull in opposite directions (Proverbs 26:4-5), because wisdom means knowing which one fits the moment. If you treat Proverbs as a guarantee, you may end up blaming yourself, or others, when life does not go to plan. Read them as wise patterns, not iron laws.

## Job and Ecclesiastes: wrestling with hard questions

The Bible itself knows that life does not always follow the patterns of Proverbs.

**Job** tells of a righteous man who loses almost everything. His friends insist he must have sinned, because suffering always means punishment. Job refuses to accept their tidy answers and cries out to God. In the end, God speaks, not to explain everything, but to show his greatness and care, and he declares the friends wrong (Job 42:7). The book teaches that suffering is not always a sign of guilt.

**Ecclesiastes** is narrated by "the Teacher," who examines work, pleasure, wealth and wisdom and finds them all fleeting, like vapour or breath. It can sound gloomy, but it is deeply honest. It ends by urging the reader to honour God and keep his commands (Ecclesiastes 12:13), and along the way it encourages enjoying simple gifts like food, work and friendship as from God's hand.

## Song of Songs

Song of Songs is a collection of love poems celebrating romance and desire between a man and a woman. It reminds us that God made human love and that it is good.

Through history, Jewish and Christian readers have also read it as a picture of God's love for his people, or Christ's love for the church. Many early church writers and much Catholic and Orthodox tradition especially treasure this spiritual reading, and many Protestant preachers of earlier centuries loved it too. Many modern readers stress the plain, human meaning first. The two readings need not compete.

## Reading poetry well

Slow down. Read aloud. Notice the pairs of lines, the pictures and the feelings. Ask what the poem wants you to feel and how it wants you to pray. Poetry is not a puzzle to solve so much as a song to join.
