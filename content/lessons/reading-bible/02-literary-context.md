If someone overheard you say, "I could kill for a cup of tea," and reported only the words "I could kill," they would give a very wrong impression. Words take their meaning from the words around them. Writers call this [[context]], and it is the first and most useful tool for reading the Bible well.

## Read paragraphs, not just verses

Many of us first meet the Bible through single verses: on a card, a mug or a social media post. Those verses are often lovely. But reading only one verse is a bit like watching a single frame of a film. You see something, but you miss the story.

A better habit is to read at least a whole paragraph, the complete thought the writer was building. Most modern Bibles help by grouping verses into paragraphs and adding small section headings. (Those headings were added by editors too, so treat them as helpful signposts rather than part of the text.)

When you find a verse that stands out, ask two simple questions:

- What comes just **before** this?
- What comes just **after** this?

These two questions alone will clear up a surprising number of puzzles.

## Numbers came later

It helps to know that the Bible's writers did not use chapter and verse numbers. They wrote letters, stories, poems and sermons in flowing text.

Chapter divisions were added around the early 1200s, usually credited to Stephen Langton, a church leader in England. Verse numbers for the whole Bible came in the 1550s, through the printer Robert Estienne. These were wonderful aids for finding your place, a bit like street numbers on houses.

But sometimes the breaks fall in odd spots, splitting a thought in two. A new chapter does not always mean a new subject. So when you read, feel free to ignore the numbers and follow the flow of the writing.

## The book's purpose and flow

Beyond the paragraph lies the whole book. Each book of the Bible was written with a purpose. Luke tells us he wanted his reader to have certainty about what he had been taught (Luke 1:1-4). John says he wrote so that readers would believe Jesus is the [[messiah|Messiah]] and have life (John 20:31).

When you know a book's aim, individual passages make more sense. You start to see how each part contributes to the whole, like rooms in a house. A quick way to learn a book's purpose is to read the introduction in a study Bible, or the short intro card for each book in this app.

## Worked example: Philippians 4:13

Philippians 4:13 is one of the most quoted verses in the Bible. Paul writes that he can do all things through Christ, who gives him strength. It is printed on sports shirts and gym walls. Many people take it to mean, "With God's help, I can achieve anything I set my mind to."

Now let's read the context, Philippians 4:10-20.

**Where is Paul?** He is in custody, probably in Rome around AD 60, though some scholars suggest Ephesus or Caesarea (Philippians 1:12-14).

**What has happened?** The church in Philippi has sent him a gift to support him (4:10, 4:18).

**What does he say just before verse 13?** He thanks them, then explains that he has learned to be content in any situation: with plenty or with little, well fed or hungry (4:11-12).

**What comes just after?** He thanks them again for sharing in his troubles and assures them that God will meet their needs too (4:14-19).

So when Paul says he can do all things through Christ, the "all things" he has in mind are being hungry, being in need, being in prison, as well as having enough. His point is not "I can win any match." It is "Whatever my circumstances, Christ gives me strength to be content and faithful."

That is not a smaller meaning. It is a deeper one. It speaks to the person in a hospital bed, the person who has lost a job, and the person who has everything and is tempted to forget God. Context did not take the verse away from us; it gave it back in its true shape.

## Another quick example

Jeremiah 29:11 is another favourite, a promise that God knows his plans to give a hope and a future. Read the chapter, though, and you discover it was sent to Jewish exiles in Babylon who would have to wait seventy years (Jeremiah 29:10). It is a promise about God's long faithfulness to his people, not a guarantee of quick personal success. We will look at this passage more closely in Lesson 4.

## How to practise

Here is a simple routine you can use this week:

1. Pick a verse you know well.
2. Read the whole paragraph it sits in.
3. Read the chapter before and after, quickly, to get the flow.
4. Ask: what is the writer talking about here, and why?
5. Only then ask what it means for you.

You may find some favourite verses become richer. Occasionally you may find a verse does not mean what you thought. Both are good gifts. They mean you are hearing the Bible more clearly, and growing as a reader who can be trusted with God's word.
