When you read a novel set in Victorian London, you quickly pick up that people travel by carriage, write letters with ink, and care a great deal about manners. The author does not stop to explain; they assume you will catch on.

The Bible's writers did the same. They assumed their readers knew about farming seasons, family duties, Roman soldiers and the [[temple]] in Jerusalem. We do not always know these things, so a little background can make a big difference. This is called historical and cultural [[context]].

## A different world

The Bible was written over more than a thousand years, roughly from around 1000 BC (or earlier for some material) to around AD 100. Exact dates for many books are debated. Its world was ancient, mostly rural, and very different from modern life. A few features are worth knowing.

**Honour and shame.** In many ancient societies, your reputation in the community was one of the most precious things you had. Families gained honour through generosity, faithfulness and wise behaviour, and lost it through public failure. Many stories turn on this.

**Family and household.** People thought of themselves first as members of a family, clan and people, not mainly as individuals. A household could include parents, children, extended relatives, servants and sometimes enslaved people.

**Agriculture.** Most people farmed or herded. That is why the Bible is full of seeds, vineyards, harvests, sheep and shepherds. When Jesus talks about sowing seed, his listeners could picture every detail.

**Roman rule.** In the New Testament period, the land of [[israel|Israel]] was part of the Roman Empire. Rome collected taxes, stationed soldiers and appointed local rulers. This explains why tax collectors were often resented and why many longed for a [[messiah|Messiah]] who would set Israel free.

**The temple.** Until it was destroyed in AD 70, the temple in Jerusalem was the centre of Jewish worship, where priests offered [[sacrifice|sacrifices]]. Many passages make more sense once you picture its importance.

## How to find this out

You do not need a degree in ancient history. A few simple tools will serve you well.

- **A study Bible.** These include notes at the bottom of each page explaining background, places and customs. Several are available from Protestant, Catholic and Orthodox publishers.
- **A Bible dictionary.** Look up a person, place or idea (such as "Samaritans" or "Pharisees") and get a short, reliable explanation.
- **Bible atlases and maps.** Seeing where places are helps stories make sense.
- **Book introductions.** These summarise when and why a book was written.

A word of caution: background should *serve* the text, not replace it. If an interesting historical claim seems to overturn the plain sense of a passage, check it carefully in more than one reliable source.

## Example: the Good Samaritan

In Luke 10:25-37, an expert in the [[law|Law]] asks Jesus who counts as his neighbour. Jesus answers with a [[parable]]: a man is attacked on the road from Jerusalem down to Jericho, a steep, lonely route known for robbers. A priest and a Levite, both connected to temple worship, pass by. Then a Samaritan stops, cares for him and pays for his recovery.

To us, "Good Samaritan" sounds like a compliment. To Jesus' first hearers, the phrase would have been startling. Jews and Samaritans had a long, bitter history, with disputes over where God should be worshipped and who were the true descendants of Israel (see John 4:9). Many Jews avoided Samaritans altogether.

So Jesus was doing more than saying "be kind." He was saying that love for neighbour crosses even the deepest lines of hostility, and that the person you look down on may show you what God's love looks like. Knowing the background sharpens the point.

## Example: wine at Cana

In John 2:1-11, Jesus attends a wedding in the village of Cana. The wine runs out. To modern readers, this might seem a small problem. But village weddings could last up to a week, and hospitality was a matter of family honour. Running out would have brought real public shame on the hosts.

When Jesus turns water into wine, he is not only showing power. He is quietly protecting a family from disgrace, and providing abundantly, more than they could ever need. John calls it the first of his signs, pointing to who Jesus is.

## A careful example: head coverings

Some passages are harder. In 1 Corinthians 11:2-16, Paul discusses whether women should cover their heads when praying or prophesying in worship. Scholars point out that in Corinth, head coverings likely carried meanings about marriage, modesty and social status that we do not share.

Christians have reached different conclusions. Some churches, including some Orthodox, Anabaptist, traditional Catholic and conservative Protestant communities, continue the practice. Many others believe Paul's underlying concerns, such as order, respect and avoiding needless offence in worship, still apply, while the specific custom was tied to that culture. This is an area to approach humbly, with your church's guidance.

## Stepping into their sandals

Learning background is a way of loving your neighbours across time. You are taking the trouble to understand their world so that you can hear what they heard. As you do, familiar passages often become vivid and surprising, just as they were the first time they were spoken.
