When people hear the word "prophecy," they often think of crystal balls and predictions. Some imagine the prophets of the Bible as mysterious figures who mainly forecast the distant future.

The reality is richer and more down to earth. The biblical [[prophet|prophets]] were, above all, God's messengers to his people in their own day.

## Covenant messengers

From the books of Isaiah through Malachi, the Old Testament contains the writings of many prophets. They lived mostly between about 800 and 400 BC, during the time of Israel's kings, the [[exile]] in Babylon, and the return home.

Think back to the [[covenant]] God made with Israel at Sinai. God promised to be their God and to bless them; they promised to love him and keep his ways. The covenant also described what would happen if Israel turned away: hardship, defeat and eventually exile (Deuteronomy 28).

The prophets were like covenant messengers. They reminded Israel of its promises. They called out injustice, idolatry and empty worship. They warned of the consequences written into the covenant. And they held out God's mercy and future hope.

Listen to Micah's famous summary of what God wants: to act justly, love mercy and walk humbly with God (Micah 6:8). That is the heartbeat of prophecy.

## Forthtelling and foretelling

A helpful pair of words:

- **Forthtelling** means speaking God's word *forth* into the present situation: "This is what God says about how you are living now."
- **Foretelling** means predicting what will happen.

Most prophecy is forthtelling. Relatively little is prediction of events far in the future, and even predictions usually serve a present purpose: to call people to [[repentance]] or to give them hope.

So when you read a prophet, start by asking: what was happening in this prophet's day, and what was God saying to the people then? A study Bible introduction will tell you who was king and what crisis the nation faced.

## Poetic imagery

Much prophecy is written as poetry, full of vivid pictures. Mountains melt, the sun goes dark, the stars fall, trees clap their hands. These images often describe God's judgment or salvation in dramatic, cosmic language, rather than literal astronomy.

For example, Isaiah 13 describes the stars and sun going dark, but the passage is about the fall of Babylon (13:1, 13:19). This is the prophet's way of saying "a world is ending" for that empire. Reading prophetic poetry well means letting the imagery do its work without forcing every detail into a literal timeline.

## Near and far fulfilment

Some prophecies had a meaning in the prophet's own time and a fuller meaning later.

Isaiah 7:14 is a well-known example. Isaiah gave King Ahaz a sign about a young woman (or virgin; translations differ) who would bear a son named Immanuel, "God with us." The sign concerned Ahaz's situation in the 700s BC. Yet Matthew sees its fullest meaning in the birth of Jesus (Matthew 1:22-23). Many Christians understand this as God weaving a pattern that reaches its height in Christ; others read the verse mainly as a direct prediction of Jesus' birth.

## Conditional prophecy: Jonah

The key passage, Jonah 3:1-10, shows something important. God sends Jonah, a reluctant prophet, to Nineveh, a great city of the cruel Assyrian empire. Jonah's message is short and blunt: in forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown.

But the people of Nineveh, from the king down, believe God. They fast, put on sackcloth and turn from their violence. And God relents. He does not bring the disaster (3:10).

Was Jonah's prophecy wrong? No. Many warnings carry an unspoken "unless you change." God explains this principle in Jeremiah 18:7-8: if a nation he has warned turns from its evil, he will not bring the harm he spoke of. Warnings are invitations to repent. That is why Jonah, who wanted Nineveh destroyed, was angry. He knew God was gracious and compassionate (Jonah 4:2).

## Fulfilled in Jesus

The New Testament writers were convinced that the prophets ultimately pointed to Jesus. Jesus himself, after his [[resurrection]], explained to two disciples how Moses and all the prophets spoke about him (Luke 24:27).

The prophets spoke of a coming king from David's line (Isaiah 9:6-7), a suffering servant who would bear the sins of many (Isaiah 53), a [[new-covenant|new covenant]] written on hearts (Jeremiah 31:31-34), and the pouring out of God's Spirit (Joel 2:28-32). Christians see each of these fulfilled in Jesus and the early church.

Sometimes fulfilment is direct prediction. Often it is a pattern or theme that reaches its fullest expression in Jesus, the true [[messiah|Messiah]].

## A brief word on the end times

Some prophecies look toward the final renewal of all things. Christians agree that Jesus will return, that the dead will be raised, and that God will make all things new. These are confessed in the ancient [[creed|creeds]] shared by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches.

But Christians hold different views on the details, such as how to understand the "thousand years" of Revelation 20, or whether some prophecies about Israel are still awaiting fulfilment. Catholic and Orthodox churches, and many Protestants, read the thousand years largely as symbolic of Christ's present reign. Many [[evangelical]] Christians expect a more literal future sequence of events. Sincere believers differ here, and it is wise to hold the details humbly while holding firmly to the shared hope.

## Reading the prophets well

Ask: who was the prophet speaking to, and what was happening? Is this a warning, a call to repent, or a promise of hope? How is the imagery working? And how does this message find its fullest meaning in Jesus? Read this way, the prophets speak with fresh urgency today.
