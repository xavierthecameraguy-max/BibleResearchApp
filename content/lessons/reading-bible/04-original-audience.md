Imagine reading a text message on someone else's phone that says, "Don't worry, I'm on my way." Who is on their way? Where? Why might the other person be worried? Without knowing who sent it and who received it, the words float free.

Every part of the Bible had a sender and a first set of receivers. Paying attention to that original audience is one of the clearest ways to understand what a passage means.

## Who first received it, and why?

When you open a book of the Bible, it is worth asking:

- Who were the first people to hear or read this?
- What was going on in their lives?
- Why did the writer think they needed these words?

Sometimes the Bible tells us directly. Paul's letters open by naming the church they are written to. The prophets often say which king was reigning. Other times we rely on clues in the text and the careful work of historians. A study Bible introduction will usually summarise what is known, and will say honestly when scholars are unsure.

## Them first, then us

The key habit is to ask "What did it mean to *them*?" before "What does it mean to *us*?"

This is not because the Bible has nothing to say to us. Quite the opposite. Christians believe God speaks through Scripture today. But a passage cannot mean something completely different for us than it meant for them. The meaning God gave through the writer is the anchor, and our application grows out of it.

Think of it as building a bridge. On one side stands the original audience. On the other side, you. The bridge is a **bridging principle**: a truth in the passage that was true for them and is still true for us. Find that, and you can cross safely.

## Example: the exiles in Jeremiah 29

Around 597 BC, the Babylonian army took many of Jerusalem's leading citizens into [[exile]], hundreds of miles from home. Some false [[prophet|prophets]] were telling them not to worry; they would be home in two years (see Jeremiah 28).

Jeremiah sent them a letter with a very different message (Jeremiah 29:1-14). Build houses, he said. Plant gardens. Marry and have children. Seek the good of the city where you live and pray for it, because its welfare will be yours. The exile would last about seventy years (29:10).

Then comes the famous promise: God knows the plans he has for them, plans for their welfare and not for harm, to give them a future and a hope (29:11).

Now notice what that means for the first audience. Many of them would grow old and die in Babylon. The promise was not that life would be easy soon. It was that God had not abandoned his people, and that he would bring them home in his own time.

What is the bridging principle? Not "God guarantees my personal plans will succeed this year." Rather: God remains faithful to his people even in long seasons of hardship; he calls them to live faithfully and do good where they are; and his purposes for them are ultimately good. That truth reaches us powerfully, especially in seasons that feel like exile.

## Example: Paul writing to Corinth

Corinth was a busy, wealthy port city in Greece, famous for trade, competition and a mix of religions. The church there, founded by Paul around AD 50, was lively but troubled. Members were splitting into factions, taking each other to court, and arguing about food offered to idols and how to behave in worship.

When Paul writes 1 Corinthians, he is answering their specific questions and problems. Some of his instructions address situations we rarely face, such as eating meat from pagan temples (1 Corinthians 8). But the bridging principle is clear: use your freedom in a way that builds up others rather than causing them to stumble. That applies to many choices we make today.

When you read Paul's famous words about love in 1 Corinthians 13, it helps to remember he was writing to a proud, divided church. It is not first a wedding reading. It is a loving rebuke to people who valued impressive gifts more than each other.

## The five study questions

Throughout this app, you will see five simple questions to ask of any passage. They gather together what this track teaches:

1. **Genre:** What kind of writing is this? (Story, poetry, law, letter, prophecy...) See [[genre]].
2. **Before and after:** What comes just before and just after?
3. **Original audience:** Who first received it, and what did it mean to them?
4. **The big story:** Where does it fit in the Bible's overall story, from creation to [[new-creation|new creation]], centred on Jesus?
5. **For me today:** In light of all that, what does God want me to believe, feel or do?

Notice that question five comes last. It is not less important; it is the goal. But it is answered best when the first four have done their work.

## Reading with respect

Paying attention to the original audience is an act of respect: for the people who first received these words, for the writers who wrote them, and for God who spoke through them. It also protects us. When we know what a passage meant then, we are much less likely to twist it into something it never said.

And very often, as with the exiles in Babylon, we discover that their situation was closer to ours than we expected.
