Imagine finding an old letter in your grandmother's attic. It begins, "My dear Ruth," talks about rationing, mentions someone called Tom who is "away," and ends with a promise to "be home by Christmas." You are not Ruth, and you have never met Tom. Yet you can still learn a great deal from that letter, if you take time to understand who wrote it, when, and why.

Reading the Bible is a bit like that. It is God's word for you, and it is also a collection of real writings that first reached real people long ago. Learning to read it well means holding both of those truths together.

## Everyone interprets

Some people say, "I don't interpret the Bible, I just read what it says." It sounds humble, but it is not quite possible. The moment you read a sentence, your mind starts deciding what the words mean, which ones matter most, and how they connect to your life. That is interpretation.

So the question is not *whether* we interpret, but whether we interpret *well*. The good news is that reading well is not a secret reserved for experts. It is a set of habits anyone can learn, and this track will walk you through them one step at a time.

## What did the author mean?

The single most important habit is this: before asking "What does this mean to me?", ask "What did the writer mean when he wrote it?"

That order matters. If we jump straight to our own feelings, we can easily make a passage say almost anything. If we first listen for what the author intended, we have something solid to stand on. Then we can ask, with confidence, how that meaning speaks to our lives today.

Christians believe the Bible is inspired by God (see [[inspiration]]). God worked through human writers, with their own languages, personalities and situations. So listening carefully to the human author is one of the main ways we listen to God.

## Two words worth knowing

Bible teachers often use two Greek-based words to describe two very different ways of reading.

- **[[exegesis|Exegesis]]** means "leading out." It is drawing out of a passage the meaning that is really there.
- **[[eisegesis|Eisegesis]]** means "leading in." It is bringing our own ideas to a passage and reading them into it, so that the Bible ends up agreeing with whatever we already believed.

All of us drift into eisegesis sometimes, often without noticing. The cure is not to stop reading but to slow down and let the text speak on its own terms.

The wider study of how to interpret well is called [[hermeneutics]]. It sounds technical, but it simply means "the principles of interpretation." You are learning hermeneutics right now.

## Written for us, but not to us

Here is a helpful phrase to remember: the Bible was written *for* us, but not *to* us.

Paul's letters were sent to churches in places like Rome and Corinth. The prophets spoke to ancient Israel. The Gospels were written for early communities of believers in the first century. None of these writers had your name on the envelope.

And yet Christians believe God intended these writings to guide his people in every age (2 Timothy 3:16-17). So we read like someone looking over the shoulder of the first readers. We learn what God was saying to them, and then we discover how that same message applies to us.

This is not a way of keeping the Bible at arm's length. It is a way of taking it seriously, the way you would take a real letter seriously.

## A picture from Nehemiah

The key passage for this lesson, Nehemiah 8:1-8, is set around 445 BC. The Jewish people had returned from [[exile]] in Babylon and gathered in Jerusalem. Ezra the [[scribe]] stood on a wooden platform and read from the Law of Moses from early morning until midday.

But reading was not all that happened. Levites, who were helpers in Israel's worship, helped the people understand, explaining the meaning so that everyone could grasp what was read (Nehemiah 8:7-8). Many of them may have spoken [[aramaic|Aramaic]] by then rather than [[hebrew|Hebrew]], so some explaining was needed.

Notice three things. The people treated the reading as important; they stood up when the book was opened. The reading was done in community, not alone. And understanding was the goal. The words were meant to be grasped, not just heard.

## You are not alone

Interpreting the Bible can feel daunting at first. Four things help enormously.

**Humility.** Come ready to be surprised and corrected. If a passage seems to say exactly what you hoped, it is worth checking again.

**Prayer.** Christians believe the Holy Spirit, who inspired Scripture, also helps believers understand and apply it. Ask God for understanding before you read.

**The church.** You read alongside believers around the world and across two thousand years. Catholic and [[orthodox|Orthodox]] Christians place strong emphasis on reading Scripture within the church's living tradition and teaching. [[protestant|Protestant]] Christians stress that Scripture is the final authority (see [[sola-scriptura]]), yet most also value the creeds, wise teachers and the shared reading of the [[church]]. Whatever your tradition, no believer was meant to interpret the Bible entirely alone.

**Patience.** Some passages are clear the first time. Others take years to understand. That is normal.

## What comes next

In the lessons ahead you will learn to look at what comes before and after a verse, to step into the ancient world, to think about the first audience, and to recognise different kinds of writing, from stories and laws to poems, prophecies, Gospels, letters and visions. By the end you will have a simple five-question method you can use with any passage.

For now, carry one question with you every time you open your Bible: *What did the writer want his first readers to understand?* It is a small question that will open up a great deal.
