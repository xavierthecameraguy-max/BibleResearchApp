Have you ever read someone else's mail? Not snooping, but perhaps a letter in a museum, or one written by a famous person to a friend. You quickly realise you are only hearing half of a conversation. The writer refers to people, events and earlier messages that you know nothing about.

That is exactly the situation with the New Testament letters, sometimes called [[epistle|epistles]]. Twenty-one of the twenty-seven New Testament books are letters. Learning how to read them well will help you with a large part of the New Testament.

## Written for an occasion

Scholars describe these letters as **occasional** documents. That does not mean "occasionally read." It means each was written for a particular *occasion*: a problem in a church, a question someone asked, a visit being planned, a gift to thank someone for, or false teaching to answer.

Paul wrote Galatians because teachers were insisting that Gentile Christians must be circumcised and keep the Jewish law to be fully accepted by God. He wrote 1 Thessalonians partly to comfort believers worried about Christians who had died. He wrote Philippians partly to thank the church for a gift.

Because of this, letters are not textbooks of theology arranged by topic. They are pastoral messages, full of rich teaching, applied to real people.

## Read the whole letter in one sitting

Here is one of the simplest and most helpful things you can do: read the whole letter in one go. Most are short. Philippians takes about fifteen minutes. Philemon takes two or three. Even Romans can be read in about an hour.

The first readers would have heard the entire letter read aloud in their gathering. Reading it all at once lets you see the flow of the argument, the main concerns and how the parts fit together. Then you can go back and study smaller sections.

It also helps to know how ancient letters were usually structured: the sender's name, the recipients, a greeting, often a thanksgiving or prayer, the main body, and then final greetings. When Paul skips the usual thanksgiving, as he does in Galatians, it is a sign of how serious the problem is.

## Mirror-reading, carefully

Because we only have one side of the conversation, we try to work out the other side from clues in the letter. This is sometimes called **mirror-reading**: looking at what the writer says and seeing what situation it reflects.

If Paul spends a long time arguing that we are put right with God by [[faith]] and not by works of the law, we can reasonably infer that someone was teaching otherwise. That is mirror-reading.

But it needs care. Not every instruction means there was a big problem. Writers sometimes repeat good advice simply because it is good. A useful rule is to rely most on what the letter says clearly and repeatedly, and to hold more speculative guesses loosely. Study Bibles and introductions can help here.

## Example: Philemon

The key passage is the whole of Philemon, Paul's shortest letter, written while he was in prison, probably in the mid-50s to early 60s AD (scholars debate where and when).

**The people.** Philemon was a Christian, probably wealthy, in whose house a church met, likely in Colossae. Onesimus had been enslaved in his household. Somehow Onesimus had come to be with Paul and had become a Christian (Philemon 1:10). Exactly how is uncertain; many think he had run away, others that he had been sent or sought Paul's help.

**The request.** Paul sends Onesimus back with this letter. He could command Philemon, he says, but he would rather appeal to him in love (1:8-9). He asks Philemon to receive Onesimus no longer simply as a slave, but as a dear brother (1:16), to welcome him as he would welcome Paul (1:17), and to charge any debt to Paul's account (1:18). He even hints that Philemon will do more than he asks (1:21).

**The principle.** The [[gospel]] changes relationships. In Christ, master and slave become brothers. Their shared life in the [[church]] puts the social hierarchy of the Roman world under deep pressure. Many Christians across history have seen in letters like this one seeds that later helped lead believers to oppose slavery, though it is honest to admit that the church was slow and that some Christians used the Bible to defend slavery.

For us, the bridging principle might be: our new family in Christ should reshape how we treat people, especially those our society ranks below us, and reconciliation is worth taking costly steps for.

## Timeless principles and cultural applications

Letters often combine lasting principles with specific applications for their own culture. Paul tells several churches to greet one another with a holy kiss (Romans 16:16). Most Christians see the principle, warm and genuine welcome, as lasting, while the form can change. Some churches, including Orthodox, Catholic and many Anglican and Lutheran ones, keep a form of this greeting in their [[liturgy]], often called the sign of peace.

It is honest to say that Christians sometimes disagree on where the line falls. Questions about women's roles in church leadership, head coverings, or certain practices in worship are examples where faithful, Bible-loving Christians reach different conclusions. Some helpful questions are:

- Does the writer connect this instruction to creation, to the character of God, or to the gospel itself?
- Is the same teaching found consistently across the Bible?
- Did the specific practice carry a meaning in that culture that it does not have in ours?

Asking these questions humbly, with your church and alongside other traditions, will help you read wisely.

## Reading letters well

Read the whole letter. Find the occasion. Mirror-read with care. Look for the principles, and think about how they apply today. Do that, and you will find these ancient letters speaking directly into your life, almost as if they had your name on the envelope after all.
