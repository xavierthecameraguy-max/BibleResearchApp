If you have ever been read a bedtime story, you already know something important about the Bible. More of it is story, or narrative, than any other kind of writing. Genesis, Exodus, Joshua, Judges, Ruth, Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, the Gospels and Acts are mostly narrative. Story is the backbone that holds the rest of the Bible together.

That is good news. Stories draw us in. But reading biblical stories well takes a little practice.

## God is the main character

The single most helpful thing to remember is this: in the Bible's stories, God is the main character.

Abraham, Moses, Ruth, David and Peter are all important. But the deeper question of every story is: *What is God doing here?* How is he keeping his promises? How is he dealing with human [[sin]]? How is he moving his great plan forward?

When we forget this, we turn Bible stories into little moral tales about heroes to copy or villains to avoid. Sometimes there are examples to follow (Hebrews 11 commends many people's [[faith]]). But the people in these stories are usually far from perfect. The real hero is the God who keeps working through them.

## Describing is not the same as approving

A second key idea is the difference between **descriptive** and **prescriptive**.

- Descriptive means *this is what happened*.
- Prescriptive means *this is what you should do*.

Bible stories are mostly descriptive. They tell us honestly what people did, including terrible things. Abraham lies about his wife (Genesis 12:10-20). Jacob deceives his father. David commits adultery and arranges a murder (2 Samuel 11). The Bible reports these things not to recommend them but to show the truth about people, and God's patience and judgment.

So when you read a story, do not assume every action is a model. Ask: does the story itself, or the wider Bible, show this as good or bad?

## How stories work

Biblical stories use the same building blocks as any story.

**Scenes.** Stories move from one scene to the next, often with only two or three characters at a time. Notice where each scene takes place and who is present.

**Characters.** We learn about people mostly through what they do and say, rarely through direct description. When the narrator does describe someone, take note; it usually matters.

**Plot.** Most stories have a problem, rising tension, a turning point and a resolution. Ask: what is the problem here, and how is it solved?

**The narrator.** Hebrew narrators are famously brief. They seldom stop to explain the moral. Instead they show us events and trust us to think. Repeated words, surprising details and especially the speeches of characters are often where the meaning lies.

## Example: David and Goliath

The story in 1 Samuel 17 is one of the best known in the world. It is often preached as: "You have giants in your life, such as fear, debt or illness. Be brave like David and you will defeat them."

There is something true in the idea that God helps us in hard times. But let's read the story as narrative and see what it actually emphasises.

**The problem.** The Philistine army is camped against Israel. Their champion, Goliath, is enormous and heavily armed. For forty days he mocks Israel and, by implication, Israel's God (17:10, 17:16). King Saul and all the soldiers are terrified (17:11). Remember that Saul was the king meant to lead Israel in battle.

**The unlikely deliverer.** David, the youngest son, a shepherd boy, arrives with food for his brothers. He has recently been quietly anointed as the future king (1 Samuel 16). He is outraged that anyone would defy "the armies of the living God" (17:26).

**The key speeches.** Listen to what David says. To Saul, he says the Lord who rescued him from lions and bears will rescue him now (17:37). To Goliath, he says he comes in the name of the Lord, so that all the earth may know there is a God in Israel, and that the battle is the Lord's (17:45-47).

**The resolution.** With a sling and a single stone, David defeats Goliath. Israel's army, frozen with fear, now rushes forward to victory.

So what is the story about? Above all, it is about God, who saves his people when they cannot save themselves, often through someone weak and unexpected. It also shows David as God's chosen king, in contrast to the fearful Saul.

Read in the light of the whole Bible, Christians have long seen a pattern here that points to Jesus: a humble, unlikely king who fights the battle his people cannot fight, and whose victory they then share.

In that story, most of us are not David. We are more like the frightened Israelites, rescued by a champion and then invited to join in his victory. That is far more encouraging than being told to try harder.

## Reading stories well

Next time you read a Bible story, try these questions:

- What is God doing in this story?
- What is the problem, and how is it resolved?
- What do the characters' words reveal?
- Is this action described, approved or condemned?
- How does this story connect to the bigger story of God's promises, fulfilled in Jesus?

Stories shape us gradually. As you read them this way, you will find yourself getting to know not just the characters, but the God who stands at the centre of them all.
