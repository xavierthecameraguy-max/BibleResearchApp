For many new readers, the books of Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy are where Bible reading plans go to die. After the drama of the [[exodus]], suddenly there are pages of rules about sacrifices, skin diseases, mildew and fabric. What are we supposed to do with all this?

The good news is that these laws make much more sense once you understand where they came from and what they were for.

## Law inside a relationship

The first five books of the Bible are called the [[pentateuch|Pentateuch]] or, in Jewish tradition, the [[torah|Torah]]. "Torah" is often translated "law," but it really means something closer to "instruction" or "teaching."

Notice the order of events. God first rescues Israel from slavery in Egypt. Only then, at Mount Sinai, does he give them his Law. The Ten Commandments begin, "I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2). Grace comes first; instruction follows.

So the Law was not a ladder for earning God's love. It was the shape of life for a people God had already saved. It was given as part of a [[covenant]], a binding relationship between God and Israel, a bit like marriage vows. God promised to be their God, and they promised to live as his people.

## Different kinds of law

The laws of the Torah cover many areas of life. Broadly, you will find:

- **Laws about worship:** the [[tabernacle]], priests, festivals and [[sacrifice|sacrifices]].
- **Laws about purity:** what made a person clean or unclean, including food laws.
- **Laws for society:** property, damages, courts, care for the poor, how to treat servants and foreigners.
- **Basic moral commands:** do not murder, do not steal, honour your parents.

Some laws are stated as clear commands ("You shall not..."). Others are case laws: "If someone does this, then that should happen." Case laws give examples to show principles of justice in action.

Many of these laws were remarkably humane for their time. Others reflect an ancient setting that can feel strange or hard to us. It is honest to admit that, and wise to read them in their setting, as part of a long story that God was still moving forward.

## How do Christians relate to the Law?

This is a big question, and Christians have answered it in more than one way. All agree that Christians are not saved by keeping the Law, and that Jesus fulfils the Law (Matthew 5:17). They differ on exactly how the Law applies now.

**The threefold division.** A very common approach, found in Catholic teaching (classically set out by Thomas Aquinas, building on earlier writers) and in Reformed and [[anglican|Anglican]] confessions, sorts the laws into three groups:

- *Moral* laws, such as the Ten Commandments, reflect God's unchanging character and still guide Christians.
- *Civil* laws governed Israel as a nation and are not directly binding on other nations today, though they show principles of justice.
- *Ceremonial* laws about sacrifices, priests and purity pointed ahead to Jesus and are fulfilled in him.

**Fulfilled as a whole.** Other Christians, including some Lutherans, some [[anabaptist|Anabaptists]] and those who hold to what is sometimes called new covenant theology, prefer to say the whole Mosaic Law, as a covenant, is fulfilled in Christ. Christians now live under the [[new-covenant|new covenant]] and the "law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). Old Testament laws still teach us about God and are often repeated or deepened in the New Testament, but we read them through Jesus.

[[orthodox|Orthodox]] Christians similarly emphasise that the Law was a teacher leading to Christ (Galatians 3:24), and read it in the light of his life and the church's worship.

In practice, these approaches often arrive at very similar conclusions. Christians do not offer animal sacrifices, because Jesus' death was the once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 10:10-14). Most do not keep the food laws, following Mark 7:19 and Acts 10. And all agree that commands like "do not murder" remain deeply important.

## Jesus' summary

When an expert asked Jesus which commandment was greatest, he answered with two: love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, mind and strength (Deuteronomy 6:5; Mark 12:29-31), and love your neighbour as yourself (Leviticus 19:18). Everything in the Law and the Prophets, he said, hangs on these two (Matthew 22:37-40).

That gives us a powerful lens. When reading any law, ask: how does this show love for God? How does it show love for neighbour?

## Example: Leviticus 19

Leviticus 19 is a wonderful place to see this. It begins with God's call: "Be holy, because I am holy" (19:2). Then it shows what holiness looks like in everyday life.

- When harvesting, leave the edges of your fields and the fallen grapes for the poor and the foreigner (19:9-10).
- Do not steal, lie or deceive (19:11).
- Pay workers on time, the same day (19:13).
- Do not curse the deaf or trip up the blind (19:14).
- Judge fairly, without favouring rich or poor (19:15).
- Do not hold a grudge; love your neighbour as yourself (19:18).
- Treat the foreigner as one of your own (19:33-34).

Holiness here is not just about religious ritual. It reaches into farming, business, courts and how you treat people with disabilities. Even if a Christian today does not own a field, the principle is clear: build generosity toward the vulnerable into your ordinary work.

## Reading the Law well

When you meet a law in the Old Testament, try asking: What did this mean for Israel? What does it show about God's character? How does Jesus fulfil it? And how does it help me love God and my neighbour? Read that way, even Leviticus can become surprisingly life-giving.
